Things to Know

Every compressed, load-bearing line from the course, collected. 31 of them. Each links back to the lesson that earns it — read this before an interview, a vendor call, or a design review.

🎯 Core Vocabulary: The Terms Every LLM System Uses

  • The Agent Loop and the Harness

    If a bigger model would fix it, it is a model problem. If better prompting, better tool results or more turns would fix it, it is a harness problem. Most of the time it is the harness — which is good news, because the harness is the part you control.

  • Weights, GGUF and Quantization

    Prefer the bigger model, more heavily quantized. A 4-bit 70B almost always beats a 16-bit 13B of similar file size. Precision is the cheaper thing to give up.

  • Serving Locally: Ollama, llama.cpp and tok/s

    When a performance number is quoted, ask which phase it measures and whether that phase is your bottleneck. A figure measured on the wrong phase points your optimisation work at the wrong layer entirely.

  • Embeddings, Vectors and Semantic Routing

    Try keyword search before you reach for a vector database. On short, jargon-heavy, identifier-rich text it frequently wins, and it costs nothing to test. This is the dumb-baseline question applied to retrieval.

  • Tool Calls and MCP

    The model chooses what to run, and its choice is influenced by every piece of text in its context — including text that arrived from outside. That is why tool descriptions are prompt engineering and why retrieved content is an attack surface.

  • The Eval Battery

    Twenty real tasks from your own work, with known-good outcomes, is the most valuable evaluation asset you can own. Small enough to build in an afternoon, specific enough that no vendor has optimised for it.

  • Overfitting and Underfitting

    Any time you tune against results you can see, you risk fitting the measurement rather than the thing measured. The defence never changes: keep some data where you cannot look at it.

  • Agents, Autonomy and the Danger Surface

    Everything an agent reads is data, not instructions. Defence is architectural — limit what the tools can do — not a matter of prompting the model to be careful.

  • Reinforcement, Feedback and Humans in the Loop

    Match the human's position to reversibility, not to how much you trust the model. Reversible and cheap: out of the loop is fine. Irreversible or outward-facing: a human approves first, however well it has behaved so far.

  • Context Engineering: What Goes In Decides What Comes Out

    Put the instruction and the most relevant material at the start or the end — never the middle. Attention over long context is U-shaped, so ranking matters more than recall.

  • Structured Output: JSON You Can Actually Parse

    Valid JSON is not correct JSON. Schema validation catches shape. Only your own checks catch sense — a perfectly-formed object can carry an invented ID and a confidence score attached to a guess.

  • Hallucination and Grounding

    Fluency and accuracy are independent. Confident phrasing carries no information about correctness, because confident phrasing is what the training data looks like. The model seeming sure is never evidence.

  • Class Imbalance and Choosing a Threshold

    Compute the trivial baseline before reporting any accuracy figure. If your model is at 99.2% and always-guess-the-majority scores 99.1%, you have not built anything.

  • Chain-of-Thought and Reasoning Tokens

    Chain-of-thought is not the model becoming smarter — it is the model being given room to compute. Which is why it helps on multi-step problems and does nothing for recall.

  • Loops at Inference: Control Flow, Limits and Dangers

    A loop improves the result only if signal enters from outside the loop. A test, a compiler, a schema, or the independence of separate samples. A loop with no external signal mostly amplifies what the model already believed, including its errors.

  • Loops in Training: Flywheels and Model Collapse

    Something outside the model's own opinion must decide what gets kept. A verifier, a human, a game result, a written constitution. Remove the filter and you are training on your own unfiltered output.

  • MCP: The Danger Surface

    Trust in MCP is a property of the server, never of what the server says about itself. Tool descriptions and annotations claiming a tool is safe are untrusted unless the server is.

  • Local Hardware: CUDA, Metal and What Actually Constrains You

    Capacity is a hard wall; bandwidth is only a slope. A model that does not fit does not run at any speed. A model that fits but runs slowly still runs. Check that it fits before you compare tokens per second.

  • Local Hardware: CUDA, Metal and What Actually Constrains You

    What blocks you is usually neither memory nor speed — it is library support. CUDA-only kernels do not run on Metal regardless of how much memory you have. Check the library before you check the chip.

⚙️ Algorithms That Matter Here

  • Gradient Descent and Backpropagation

    Overfit a single batch before anything else. A correctly-wired network drives eight examples to near-zero loss trivially. If it cannot, the bug is in the loop or the data, and no hyperparameter will find it.

  • k-Means, k-NN and the Curse of Dimensionality

    Clusters are always returned, whether or not they exist. k-means partitions; it does not detect structure. Check that the same k on shuffled data looks meaningfully worse before believing a clustering.

  • Data Leakage

    Overfitting announces itself as a gap; leakage lifts both curves together. A surprisingly good result on a hard problem is more likely a leak than a breakthrough — treat it as a bug report.

🧪 Recipes, Decisions and Harnesses

  • Why Measurement Decides

    An instrument that cannot tell success from inaction produces noise, not numbers. Prove it fails before the work and passes after it, before you trust anything it tells you.

  • SWE-bench, and How to Actually Run It

    A benchmark must make doing nothing and solving it score differently. FAIL_TO_PASS proves the work happened; PASS_TO_PASS proves nothing else broke. Most homemade benchmarks omit the second half and quietly reward vandalism.

  • Confounds You Cannot See

    A limit that can end a run and cannot be seen is not a guard, it is a confound. An invisible terminator does not merely stop the run — it stops it while looking exactly like the thing you were trying to measure.

  • Models Judging Models

    Ask not how accurate your grader is, but whether its error correlates with what you changed. Noise costs you power. Bias that tracks the treatment invents an effect, and more sampling will not remove it.

  • Deterministic First

    A model is the most expensive, slowest, least debuggable and least testable component you can put in a system. Sometimes it is the only thing that can do the job. Frequently it is not.

  • Vibe Coding and Spec-Driven Development

    An agent cannot tell a decision you made from a sentence you wrote carelessly. Every under-specified area becomes an invented answer, delivered confidently. Vagueness surfaces as working code, not as questions.

🔍 Block 1 · Python, Data and EDA

  • Tabular Data and the Lies dtypes Tell

    A numeric-looking column that loads as text is a question, not a detail. It means something non-numeric is in there. Ten seconds of reading dtypes routinely saves an afternoon.

  • Missing Data: The Mechanism Matters More Than the Amount

    Why a value is missing matters more than how many are missing. Under MNAR the median fill replaces your most alarming readings with typical ones, hiding exactly the risk you were hired to find.

  • AI-Assisted Analysis, and Where It Lies to You

    Let it write the code. Do not let it make the decisions. Every entry on your cleaning-decisions list should be one you can defend, and “the model suggested it” is not a defence.
