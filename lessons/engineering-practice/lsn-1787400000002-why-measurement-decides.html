Why Measurement Decides

The only thing that corrects a harness, a recipe or a decision is a number that disagrees with it.

Everything in the previous lesson failed the same way: an artifact stayed confident after its truth expired. None of the three can notice this about itself. A harness cannot tell you its own message is misleading; a recipe cannot tell you its first step is stale; an ADR cannot tell you its evidence was overturned. Only a measurement can.

Plausible mechanisms are not findings

The failure mode is not laziness. It is the opposite — it is a good engineer reasoning carefully from a mechanism that makes sense, and shipping it. Some real examples, each of which sounded correct and was wrong:

“Mean-centering will sharpen the router”
Regressed, twice
Standard practice for embedding similarity. Accuracy fell from 88% to 70% on the first attempt and 92% to 66% on the second.
“More exemplars will help classification”
Regressed
True only when paired with a different aggregation method. Alone, it made things worse.
“This model class can’t drive tool loops”
False
A property of one model-and-runtime combination, generalised into a law that shaped months of architecture.
“The cheap tier is weak at agentic work, skip it”
False
It edited fine. The harness was strangling it with a read budget tuned for a different model.
The rule that falls out of thisIf you are about to write “this should improve X”, stop and write the measurement that would prove it instead. The sentence is the same length. One of them can be wrong without anyone noticing.

The instrument lies before the result does

There is a failure worse than a wrong conclusion: a conclusion drawn from an instrument that cannot distinguish success from doing nothing. It produces confident numbers, they move when you change things, and every one of them is noise.

A benchmark of eight coding tasks was used for months to decide model and architecture questions. An audit found that six of the eight passed their own verification at the starting commit — before any work was done. The tests that proved the feature had been added by the very change the agent was being asked to reproduce, so they were absent at the start, so the test suite passed vacuously. An agent that edited nothing scored the same as one that solved the task.

Every architectural conclusion drawn from that board had to be thrown out, including a headline result that a whole escalation mechanism produced zero benefit. It produced zero measured benefit, because the instrument could not see benefit.

Two kinds of honesty

The invariant above is outcome honesty — can the instrument tell success from inaction? There is a second one, checked far less often. Process honesty: did the instrument record what the system actually did and said, or only whether it succeeded?

Two completely different failures score identically under outcome metrics alone:

Never understood the task
edit_attempted = false
Needs better grounding, retrieval, or task framing.
Understood it and would not act
edit_attempted = false
Needs a prompt or tool change. Grounding is already fine, and every grounding fix will measure flat.

In the case that opened this module, nine consecutive experiments were run against the second failure using fixes aimed at the first — because the reasoning that would have distinguished them was being parsed off the API response and discarded on every tool-calling turn. One trace, once logging was restored, answered what nine experiments had not.

The older name for thisReading what a subject says while working, rather than only scoring the outcome, is think-aloud protocol analysis — formalised by Ericsson and Simon in the 1980s for human problem-solving. The machine-learning analogue is the distinction between process supervision and outcome supervision: scoring the steps rather than the final answer.
And the caveat that keeps it honestVerbalised reasoning is evidence, not ground truth. A model’s stated chain of thought does not always reflect the computation that produced its answer, so a trace can be sincere and still misleading. Treat it as a witness statement: extremely valuable, independently checkable, occasionally wrong.

Further reading

  • Let’s Verify Step by Step (Lightman et al., 2023)The process- versus outcome-supervision result: step-level feedback outperforms final-answer feedback.
  • Protocol Analysis: Verbal Reports as Data — Ericsson & SimonThe human-factors ancestor. Worth knowing that this method is forty years old before presenting it as new.

3. Why is a model's stated reasoning still worth logging, given it may not reflect the real computation?

It is guaranteed to be accurate for reasoning models
It is checkable evidence you otherwise do not have — and its absence guarantees you are guessing
It improves the model's accuracy
