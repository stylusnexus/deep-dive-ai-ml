Linear and Logistic Regression

The two algorithms you should try before anything clever — and the baseline everything else must beat.

These are the workhorses. They're also the dumb baseline your intake sheet demands, which makes them the most useful algorithms in this lesson set — not because they win often, but because they tell you whether winning was hard.

Linear regression predicts a number

Fit a straight line (or plane, in more dimensions) through the data so that squared error is smallest. Each input gets a coefficient saying how much it moves the output. That's it.

The underrated property is interpretability: the coefficients are readable. "Each extra year of equipment age adds 0.4 to predicted failure risk" is a sentence you can take to a stakeholder. A gradient-boosted ensemble that scores two points higher gives you nothing you can say out loud.

Logistic regression predicts a probability

Despite the name, it classifies. It fits a linear combination of inputs and then squashes the result through a sigmoid into the range 0–1, giving a probability rather than a hard label.

Where the threshold livesLogistic regression outputs 0.73, not "fraud". Turning that into a decision requires a threshold, and choosing it is a business decision about the cost of false positives versus false negatives — question 6 on your intake sheet. The default of 0.5 is a convention, not an answer.

Regularised variants worth knowing by name

Ridge (L2)
Shrinks coefficients toward zero
Handles correlated inputs gracefully. Keeps every feature, just smaller.
Lasso (L1)
Drives some coefficients to exactly zero
Performs feature selection for you. Useful when you suspect most inputs are noise.
Elastic Net
Both penalties combined
The pragmatic default when you don't know which you want.

Assumptions that break quietly

  • Linearity. The relationship really is roughly a straight line. Plot it before assuming.
  • Independence. Rows don't influence each other. Time series violate this badly — consecutive days are not independent samples.
  • No extreme multicollinearity. Two nearly-identical inputs make coefficients unstable and uninterpretable, even while predictions stay fine.
  • Outlier sensitivity. Squared error means one extreme point can drag the whole line.
The thing to actually doRun logistic or linear regression first, on every tabular problem, before touching a neural network. If it gets within a couple of points of your fancy model, ship the simple one — it trains in seconds, deploys anywhere, and you can explain its decisions when someone asks.
