Decision Trees: How a Split Gets Chosen

The one algorithm you can read end to end — and the reason it overfits so eagerly.

A decision tree asks a sequence of yes/no questions about the inputs and follows the answers to a prediction. Its appeal is total transparency: you can print the tree and read exactly why any single prediction was made. No other competitive algorithm offers that.

Choosing the split

At each node the algorithm tries every feature and every threshold, and picks the split that makes the resulting groups most pure — most dominated by a single class. Purity is measured two ways in practice:

Gini impurity
Probability of mislabelling a random item
0 means perfectly pure. The scikit-learn default, and marginally faster.
Entropy
Information-theoretic disorder
The same quantity your statistics course covers. Gives near-identical trees to Gini in practice.
Variance reduction
For regression trees
Pick the split that most reduces variance within each group.

Then it recurses on each group. That's the whole algorithm — greedy, local, and never revisiting an earlier split even if a later one would have justified a different choice.

Why trees overfit so readily

Nothing in the algorithm says stop. Given enough depth a tree can isolate every training example in its own leaf, achieving perfect training accuracy and learning nothing generalisable. This is the overfitting lesson made concrete — and it's why the controls all constrain growth:

  • max_depth — how many questions deep the tree may go
  • min_samples_leaf — refuse splits that would create tiny leaves
  • min_samples_split — don't split a node with too few examples
  • Pruning — grow fully, then cut back branches that don't help validation performance
The instability nobody warns you aboutChange a handful of training rows and a decision tree can restructure completely — a different feature wins at the root and everything below changes. That variance is exactly what ensembles exist to cancel out, which is the next lesson.

What they're good and bad at

Good: mixed numeric and categorical inputs, no scaling required, non-linear relationships, missing values handled natively by some implementations, and complete interpretability. Bad: smooth relationships (they approximate a line with a staircase), extrapolation beyond the training range (impossible — they predict a constant per leaf), and stability.

A split, computed

Worth doing once by hand, because it demystifies the whole family. Take 100 turbines, 20 of which failed. Before any split, Gini impurity is 1 minus the sum of squared class proportions: 1 − (0.8² + 0.2²) = 0.32.

Now try splitting on vibration above 0.5. Suppose that puts 30 turbines on one side, 15 of them failures, and 70 on the other, 5 of them failures:

High-vibration group
30 rows, 15 failures
Gini = 1 − (0.5² + 0.5²) = 0.50. Worse than the parent — a coin flip.
Low-vibration group
70 rows, 5 failures
Gini = 1 − (0.93² + 0.07²) = 0.13. Much purer.
Weighted result
0.3 × 0.50 + 0.7 × 0.13 = 0.24
Down from 0.32. That reduction of 0.08 is the split's score.

The algorithm computes that number for every feature at every candidate threshold and keeps the best. Then it recurses. Everything else — random forests, gradient boosting, the whole tabular-ML establishment — is built on top of that one greedy calculation.

Why the split can look like it makes things worseThe high-vibration group came out at 0.50, above the parent's 0.32, and the split is still excellent. Purity is judged on the weighted average of the children, not on each child, because you have isolated 70 rows you can now be confident about. A group that is uncertain but small costs little.

Feature importance, and why it misleads

Trees report which features drove the splits, and this is one of the most trusted and most misread outputs in machine learning. The default measure — total impurity reduction attributed to each feature — is biased toward high-cardinality features. A continuous variable offers hundreds of candidate thresholds; a binary flag offers one. The continuous variable wins more splits partly on opportunity rather than on merit.

Use permutation importance when the answer mattersShuffle one feature's values and measure how much performance drops. That directly answers the question people think they are asking — does the model actually rely on this? — and it is computed on held-out data, so it reflects generalisation rather than how the tree happened to be built. Slower, and worth it whenever the importance ranking will be shown to anyone who might act on it.
