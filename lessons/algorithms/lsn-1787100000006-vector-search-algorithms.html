Lesson 7 of 9

Nearest-Neighbour Search at Scale

Exact search, HNSW, and the recall you quietly trade away for speed.

The previous lesson on k-NN described finding the nearest vectors. Doing that across ten million embeddings, in under fifty milliseconds, is a different problem — and the algorithm you choose has a failure mode most people never measure.

Exact search, and when it's fine

Compare the query against every stored vector and keep the best matches. It's exhaustive, perfectly accurate, and linear in the number of vectors. For under roughly 100,000 vectors this is genuinely fine on modern hardware — often single-digit milliseconds.

Don't optimise this earlyMost RAG corpora are far smaller than people assume. A few thousand documents chunked into tens of thousands of vectors is comfortably exact-search territory. Reach for approximate indexing when you've measured that you need it, not because the tutorial used it.

Approximate nearest neighbour

Above that scale, ANN algorithms trade a small amount of accuracy for a large amount of speed. They don't guarantee finding the true nearest neighbours — they find most of them, most of the time.

HNSW
Hierarchical navigable small world
A layered graph. Search starts in a sparse top layer that covers ground quickly, then descends into denser layers to refine. The dominant choice — fast and accurate, at the cost of memory.
IVF
Inverted file index
Cluster the vectors, then search only the nearest few clusters. Lower memory, and misses anything sitting just outside a searched cluster.
Product quantization
Compress the vectors themselves
Often paired with IVF to cut memory dramatically. Quantization again — same trade, different object.

The number nobody measures

Recall@k is the fraction of true nearest neighbours your index actually returned. An HNSW index at 95% recall silently misses one in twenty of the results it should have found — and because the results it does return look perfectly reasonable, nothing appears broken.

How this fails in productionRAG quality drops and everyone investigates the prompt, the chunking, the model. The actual cause is an index tuned for speed that never retrieves the right chunk in the first place. Measure recall against exact search on a sample before blaming anything downstream — it takes an hour and rules out an entire category of problem.

The tuning knobs trade directly: HNSW's ef_search and M raise recall and cost latency and memory. There is no setting that gives you both.

The two knobs, and what each costs

M
Connections per node, set at build time
Higher means a denser graph: better recall, more memory, slower to build. Changing it means rebuilding the index.
ef_construction
Effort spent while building
Higher builds a better graph, once, for a longer build. Cheap to be generous with — you pay it only at index time.
ef_search
Candidates examined per query
The one you tune live. Raise it for recall, pay in latency. This is the dial to move first when recall is low.
Tune ef_search before rebuilding anythingLow recall is far more often an ef_search that was left at a library default than a fundamentally wrong index. It costs nothing to raise, takes effect immediately, and its cost is latency you can measure. Only rebuild with a larger M when you have established that ef_search alone cannot reach the recall you need.

Measuring recall, in about an hour

Take a sample of real queries — two hundred is plenty. Run each through exact search over the full set and keep the true top-k. Run each through your index. Recall@k is the mean overlap. That number is the honest description of your retrieval layer, and almost nobody computes it.

Do this once at build time and re-run it on a schedule, because recall degrades as a corpus grows and shifts. An index tuned when you had 50,000 documents is not necessarily the same index at 500,000.

The other reason to keep vectors in Postgres

Specialist vector databases are faster at pure vector search. What they are not good at is the query you actually write, which almost always has a filter attached — this customer, this date range, this document type. Filtered vector search is genuinely hard: filter first and you may search too few candidates to find good neighbours; search first and you may filter away everything you found.

Filtering is where the real engineering isA database that already holds your relational data can combine a vector search with a SQL predicate in one planner. Two separate stores means doing that join yourself, in application code, badly. At the scale most teams operate, one database that does both adequately beats two that each do half the job well.
