Lesson 9 of 11

Ranking and Hybrid Retrieval

Best Matching 25 (BM25), reciprocal rank fusion and rerankers — why pure vector search is rarely the best answer.

The context-engineering lesson concluded that ranking matters more than recall — that getting the right chunk into the first position beats retrieving more chunks. This lesson is the algorithms that do the ranking.

BM25: keyword search, done properly

BM25 scores a document against a query using term frequency, adjusted two ways: rare terms count for more than common ones, and repeated occurrences give diminishing returns. It's decades old, needs no training, no GPU and no embedding model, and it is a genuinely strong baseline.

Where BM25 beats embeddings outrightExact identifiers, error codes, product SKUs, function names, file paths, proper nouns, and any rare term. Embeddings compress meaning and lose precise tokens; BM25 matches them exactly. For a technical corpus this is not a small difference — it is frequently the difference between finding the answer and not.

Why hybrid wins

Vector search finds paraphrases and misses exact terms. BM25 finds exact terms and misses paraphrases. Their failures are largely independent, which is precisely the condition under which combining two methods helps — the same logic as ensembles in lesson 4.

Reciprocal rank fusion

The problem with combining them is that a BM25 score of 14.2 and a cosine similarity of 0.83 aren't comparable quantities. Reciprocal rank fusion sidesteps this entirely by ignoring the scores and using only the ranks: each document scores the sum of 1 / (k + rank) across the lists it appears in, with k typically 60.

A document ranked highly by both methods rises to the top; one ranked highly by a single method still places well. No score normalisation, no tuning, no training. It's a two-line algorithm that reliably outperforms either input list.

Rerankers: the expensive final pass

Retrieval embeds queries and documents separately, so it can never see them together. A cross-encoder reranker does — it takes the query and one document as a single input and scores their relevance directly. Far more accurate, and far too slow to run over a whole corpus.

So the standard architecture is a funnel: retrieve 100 candidates cheaply with hybrid search, rerank those 100 properly, keep the top 5 for the model. You get cross-encoder quality at nearly bi-encoder cost, and the top-of-list precision that context engineering demands.

The pipeline worth defaulting to

1. Retrieve wide
BM25 + vector, ~50 each
Independent failure modes, so coverage is better than either alone.
2. Fuse
Reciprocal rank fusion
Two lines of code, no tuning, no normalisation.
3. Rerank
Cross-encoder over ~100
The accuracy step. Only affordable because the candidate set is small.
4. Truncate
Top 3–5 into context
Precision beats volume — distractors actively cost you.

What BM25 is actually computing

Three ideas, each fixing a flaw in the one before it.

Term frequency
A word appearing often signals relevance
But not linearly. Ten mentions is not ten times more relevant than one, so BM25 saturates — the fifth occurrence adds far less than the second.
Inverse document frequency
Rare words carry more information
A document matching "gearbox" tells you much more than one matching "the". Weight each term by how rare it is across the corpus.
Length normalisation
Long documents match everything
A 50-page manual contains most words by accident. Divide the benefit by length, tuned so that being long is penalised but not fatal.
Saturation is the part people missPlain term-frequency scoring can be gamed by repetition, and worse, it genuinely misranks — a short document that says "gearbox" once is often more relevant than a long one that says it fifteen times in passing. BM25's saturation curve is a small piece of mathematics that encodes a real judgement about how language works, and it is most of why a 1990s algorithm still competes with embeddings.

Chunking, which decides more than your retriever does

Every retrieval method here operates on chunks you chose. Choose them badly and no amount of ranking recovers.

  • Too small and a chunk lacks the context to be understood — a paragraph referring to "this threshold" with the threshold defined two chunks earlier.
  • Too large and the embedding averages several topics into a vector that represents none of them well, while burning context when retrieved.
  • Split on structure, not on character count. Headings, sections, function boundaries. A chunk that ends mid-sentence embeds badly and reads worse.
  • Overlap a little so a passage spanning a boundary is not lost by both chunks.
  • Keep the parent reference. Retrieve the chunk, then optionally expand to its section for context — small to match, larger to read.
Look at your chunks before tuning anything downstreamPrint twenty at random and read them. If you cannot tell what a chunk is about without its neighbours, your retrieval will not be able to either, and every hour spent on embedding models or rerankers is spent on the wrong layer. This takes ten minutes and it is skipped almost universally.
