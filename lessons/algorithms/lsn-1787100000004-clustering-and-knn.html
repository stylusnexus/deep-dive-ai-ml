k-Means, k-NN and the Curse of Dimensionality

Two algorithms built on distance — and the reason distance stops meaning much in high dimensions.

Both algorithms in this lesson rest on the same assumption: that things close together are similar. That assumption is powerful, and it degrades in a specific way you need to recognise, because it also affects the embeddings and vector search you'll use in Block 3.

k-means: finding groups nobody labelled

You pick k, the number of clusters. The algorithm places k centres at random, then repeats two steps until nothing moves: assign every point to its nearest centre, then move each centre to the average of its assigned points. That's the entire algorithm.

k-NN: no training at all

k-nearest neighbours doesn't build a model. To predict, it finds the k most similar training examples and takes their majority vote or average. All the work happens at prediction time, which makes training instant and inference expensive — the opposite profile from everything else here.

You already know this algorithmRAG is k-NN. Embed the query, find the nearest stored vectors, use them. The retrieval step in Block 3 is exactly this algorithm, run over embeddings instead of tabular features — which is why the failure modes below transfer directly.

The curse of dimensionality

As dimensions increase, points spread out and the distances between them converge. In very high dimensions the nearest and farthest neighbours of a point end up almost equidistant — so "nearest" carries progressively less meaning.

This is why embeddings are typically a few hundred to a couple of thousand dimensions rather than tens of thousands, why dimensionality reduction (PCA, UMAP) is worth knowing, and why hybrid retrieval — combining vector similarity with keyword matching — outperforms pure vector search on many real corpora. Pure distance is a weaker signal than it looks.

Choosing k, honestly

Two standard methods, and the honest caveat that neither is decisive.

Elbow method
Plot within-cluster variance against k
Look for where the curve bends. Real data frequently has no visible elbow, and then the method tells you nothing.
Silhouette score
How well each point fits its cluster vs the next-nearest
Ranges -1 to 1; pick the k that maximises the mean. More decisive than the elbow, and slower.
Domain constraint
Usually the real answer

When k-means is the wrong shape

  • Elongated or curved groups. k-means draws straight boundaries around round blobs. DBSCAN follows density instead and finds arbitrary shapes.
  • Very different group sizes. A large cluster tends to get split while a small one gets absorbed.
  • Outliers. Every point must join a cluster, so a few extreme values drag a centre toward them. DBSCAN can label points as noise; k-means cannot.
  • Unknown k. If k is genuinely unknown, hierarchical clustering gives you the whole tree of possible groupings to cut where you like.
