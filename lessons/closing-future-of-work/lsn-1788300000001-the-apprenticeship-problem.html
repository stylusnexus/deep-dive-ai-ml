Lesson 2 of 2

The Apprenticeship Problem

An argument, not a finding: the scarce thing becomes knowing where the boundary is, and we are automating the work that used to teach it.

Everything below is argument. It is consistent with the evidence in the previous lesson and it is not established by it, and where I think it is weak I have said so at the end rather than left it out. Read it as a position to disagree with, which is what a closing chapter should be.

The starting point: a video, and an executive who changed her mind

This chapter began with a talk by Clara Shih, formerly of Salesforce and Meta, on how AI could radically change white-collar work. Her account of the turn is specific: she watched AI agents match and in places beat her strongest people across a set of tasks, and describes feeling radicalised in the moment of seeing it work. She left to build a nonprofit aimed at helping young people find work before entry-level roles disappear, on the reasoning that the only way for people to keep pace with AI is to give them AI.

I think the observation is right and the remedy is incomplete, and the gap between those two is where this chapter lives.

The shape of the change

Put the two strongest findings from the previous lesson next to each other. Within the model's competence, the largest gains go to the least experienced — up to 36% in field experiments, minimal for the most experienced. Outside its competence, people using it were about 19 percentage points less accurate than people without it.

Read together those say something sharper than either alone. The technology compresses the skill distribution inside its frontier and steepens the penalty outside it. If that holds, the capability that becomes scarce is not the ability to produce the work — that is what got compressed — but the ability to tell which side of the boundary you are standing on.

Worth knowing

The scarce skill is knowing where the model stops being reliable, and it is the hardest thing to teach without experience.

Recognising that an answer is confidently wrong requires knowing what a right answer looks like in that domain, and that recognition is built by having been wrong and corrected, repeatedly, on work that mattered enough for someone to check. It is not transferable by instruction. This entire course is an attempt to transfer some of it and the honest assessment is that lessons can only get a reader to the point where their own mistakes start teaching them faster.

Why the junior end is not just the cheap end

Junior work has always been two things at once, and organisations bought it for the first and got the second thrown in. It is output, produced more slowly and less well than a senior would. It is also the apprenticeship — the mechanism by which someone acquires the judgement that makes them senior, through doing work slightly beyond them and having it corrected.

Automating that work removes the first function and, incidentally, the second. Nobody decides to stop training people; the training was a by-product of an economic arrangement, and the arrangement changed. Meanwhile the demand for senior judgement rises, because somebody has to review output that arrives faster than before and carries no reasoning with it — which is the problem the engineering-practice block calls code nobody decided.

Where Shih already agrees, which I initially got wrong

An earlier draft of this chapter set up a disagreement that does not exist. It rendered her remedy as “give people AI” and then argued that tools alone cannot rebuild an apprenticeship. Her own material is more careful than that: alongside the tooling, the conversation devotes a section to why beginners should not use AI to learn something new.

Her formulation is sharper than the one this chapter had. On beginners learning something new, whether creative writing or programming, her position is that you should not use AI, because “it's through that struggle and making mistakes and getting that feedback and working through and correcting those mistakes that we build the foundation that we need to become experts. And we need expertise in order to critique AI's outputs.” On why that ordering cannot be skipped: before you have that expertise, “you really are in no position to use AI, because you're delegating to something that you can't judge.”

That last clause is the whole argument in nine words, and it is hers rather than mine. It also matters as corroboration: someone who deployed this technology at scale at two large companies, with the hiring data in front of her, arrives at the position that the tool which most raises a novice's output is the wrong thing to hand them while they are acquiring judgement.

What survives of my original point is narrower and I think still stands. If beginners should not learn with the tool, and the junior tasks that used to teach them are the ones being automated, then somebody has to deliberately arrange the corrected-mistake loop that used to be a by-product of employing them. Access to tools does not do it. Neither does advice not to use them. Someone has to pay for work whose output is worse than the alternative, on purpose, because the point of it was never the output.

The most valuable thing an organisation can do is probably unfashionable

If this argument holds, the highest-leverage move available to an employer is to keep hiring juniors and deliberately spend senior time correcting them, while accepting that the same output could be produced faster another way. That is a cost with no visible return in the quarter it is paid, which is precisely why it will not happen by default and why the shortfall shows up years later as an absence nobody can attribute.

How far the evidence carries this, and where it stops

Balance for its own sake is a distortion too, so it is worth being precise about which parts of this argument the data supports and which are extrapolation. They are not the same, and treating them alike would be its own kind of dishonesty.

The compositional claim — well supported
Four independent lines, one direction
Junior employment down 8 to 10 per cent in adopting firms (Hosseini and Lichtinger, US); an early-career shortfall against less-exposed peers in AI-exposed occupations (Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, US); US graduate unemployment up 1.6 points in three years (New York Federal Reserve); a quarter of employers reporting reduced entry-level task need (a small US recruiter survey); and UK firms with high exposure slowing junior hiring (Klein Teeselink). Different countries, methods and instruments, pointing the same way. Strong enough to state as a finding — while noting that three of the five are working papers or surveys rather than peer-reviewed work, and that the Stanford figures are explicitly descriptive rather than causal.
The frontier asymmetry — well supported
And it is the load-bearing observation
Largest gains for the least experienced inside the model's competence (36 per cent for the lowest-skill quintile in Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond), and a 19-point accuracy penalty outside it (Dell'Acqua and colleagues). Two studies rather than a literature, and the second rests on a single task, so hold the direction more firmly than the magnitudes.
The mechanism — formally modelled
And the model says something sharper than this chapter did
Ide's task-based overlapping-generations model of novice-expert knowledge transmission (CEPR DP20940) finds that improvements in entry-level automation raise output on adoption but can reduce growth and welfare even without reducing entry-level employment. The damage does not require job losses; it requires novices being reallocated away from the most productive experts, which slows the diffusion of best practice.
Magnitude and endpoint — unsupported
The confident do not know either
How large, how fast and where it settles are not in this data. The productivity figures include a forecast and a self-report gap the research itself flags. Anyone quoting a number for 2030 is guessing, and this chapter does not have one.
Worth knowing

The entry point is narrowing while the total holds. That much is a finding; what it means for 2030 is not.

The strongest honest position is narrower than the argument on either side. It is not that AI is destroying employment — the aggregate says otherwise, in two countries, clearly. It is not that nothing is happening — the junior-senior split says otherwise, from four directions. It is that the composition is shifting against the entry point, that the entry point was also the training mechanism, and that nobody has yet shown what replaces it.

The refinement that makes the argument harder to dismiss

The chapter as first drafted argued from junior job losses, and that turns out to be the weaker version. Ide's model assumes that joint production is necessary for skill acquisition — a novice executing routine tasks alongside an expert gains direct insight into how that expert turns inputs into output, “unconsciously learning the rules of the art”. On that account the transmission depends on who you work beside, not merely on being employed.

Which means the reassuring statistic is not reassuring. If junior headcount holds steady but the work is restructured so novices no longer sit alongside the best practitioners, the mechanism breaks anyway and the employment figures show nothing at all. This is the same reading problem as the aggregate-versus-composition finding, one level deeper: a number can hold while the thing it was standing in for disappears underneath it.

The design of the automation decides the sign

The same model contains the hopeful half, and it is actionable rather than consoling. Technological improvements that increase the number of novices learning from top experts strengthen transmission and raise growth. So this is not an argument that automation harms learning — it is an argument that automation which reallocates novices away from the best experts does, and that automation which brings more of them into contact with the best does the opposite. That is a design decision somebody makes, usually without knowing they are making it.

The evidence outside economics points the same way. Beane's ethnography of robotic surgery (Administrative Science Quarterly, 2019) documents surgical residents losing the hands-on participation that built skill, and resorting to informal workarounds he calls shadow learning to get it back. That is the mechanism observed directly, in a domain with nothing to do with software, where the stakes make it very hard to argue the apprenticeship was ornamental.

The strongest objection, which does not go away

Every previous automation of professional work produced exactly this prediction and it was wrong each time. Compilers were going to end programming and produced more programmers. Spreadsheets were going to gut accounting and the profession grew. Computer-aided design did not empty the drawing offices. In each case the apprenticeship reformed around the tool rather than disappearing, and those forecasting otherwise reasoned precisely as this chapter does — from the tasks being automated to the skills that would stop being learned.

I take that seriously and it does not move me all the way, for one specific reason. Those tools automated the tedious part of junior work and left the judgement-forming part behind: a compiler removed the manual translation and left you debugging your own logic. What is being automated now is the whole task, including the part where you were wrong and found out. That is a difference in kind rather than degree, and it is the only reason I weight the present case above the historical prior rather than below it.

Unemployment does not have to rise much for the harm to be real

This defuses the most common dismissal. Aggregate unemployment barely moved through the manufacturing shock in the American rust belt, and the damage to the places and cohorts absorbing it was severe and durable. A national figure that stays flat is entirely compatible with one cohort — here, people entering work now — bearing a concentrated cost. “The unemployment rate is fine” is not the reassurance it sounds like.

The organisational version, which is speculation and marked as such

A harder possibility raised in the same conversation: that the way a person conceives of what a job should entail may not be the optimal way to construct a job, and that this could apply at the level of a whole organisation. Marketing, sales, customer service, finance, HR are human-designed constructs of fairly recent vintage. If general methods with enough compute tend to beat hand-crafted structure — the argument this course already accepts about hand-engineered features in a model — the same reasoning might apply to hand-engineered departments in a company.

There is no evidence for this whatsoever, which is why it sits in a callout rather than in the argument. Hold it as a possibility, not a forecast.

What follows for someone who has read this far

This course has an implicit answer to the question and it is worth making explicit, because it shaped what was taught. Almost nothing here was about producing output faster. It was about knowing what to measure, noticing when a measurement is lying, telling a real difference from a wobble, checking whether the model was needed at all, and recognising the failures that arrive looking like successes.

That was a bet: that the durable skill is judgement about systems rather than fluency at producing them, and that judgement is built by being wrong on purpose in cheap places. Every fixture that failed before the work, every baseline written down before the run, every measurement that turned out to be measuring the instrument — those were rehearsals for being wrong somewhere expensive. If the argument in this chapter is right, that is the correct bet. If the historical prior is right, it is still not a bad one.

Be wrong on purpose, in cheap places, on a schedule

If the corrected-mistake loop is no longer supplied by the job, it has to be arranged deliberately. Build the thing before reading how. Predict the number before running the eval. Write down what you expect and check. This course is full of moments where a check disagreed with a confident expectation, and every one of those was worth more than a lesson that went smoothly — which is the whole argument in miniature.

Further reading

  • Canaries in the Coal Mine? (Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, Stanford Digital Economy Lab)The early-career employment shortfall, from ADP payroll data. Read the authors' own framing: they call these early descriptive indicators rather than causal estimates, and the headline figure has moved between data vintages.
  • Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change (Hosseini and Lichtinger)US resume and job-posting data on 62 million workers. The junior-versus-senior split, at 8-10 per cent depending on specification. A working paper, not peer-reviewed.
  • Large Language Models, Small Labor Market Effects (Humlum and Vestergaard, NBER 33777)Danish administrative data. Precise zeros on earnings and hours across eleven exposed occupations, with intervals ruling out effects above one per cent.
  • Tracking the Impact of AI on the Labor Market (The Budget Lab at Yale)The US economy-wide picture, updated continuously. Cite the current version rather than a snapshot, because it moves.
  • The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates (Federal Reserve Bank of New York)The graduate unemployment series. Note the definition: aged 22 to 27, bachelor's or higher, not currently enrolled, US only.
  • Generative AI at Work (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, NBER 31161)The customer-support study behind the skill gradient. 5,172 agents, a staggered rollout rather than a randomised experiment. Published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025.
  • Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier (Dell'Acqua et al.)758 consultants, and the task deliberately placed outside the model's reach. The source of the 19-point figure, and of the spread behind it.
  • Macroeconomic productivity gains from AI in G7 economies (OECD, 2025)The sector projections. Cumulative over ten years and modelled rather than observed, which is the part most often dropped when these figures are repeated.
  • The Simple Macroeconomics of AI (Acemoglu, NBER 32487)The considerably more conservative estimate, and worth reading against the OECD's. That two credible estimates differ by an order of magnitude is itself the finding.
