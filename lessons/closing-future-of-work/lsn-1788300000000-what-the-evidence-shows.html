What the Evidence Actually Shows

Before anyone's thesis, including mine: the measured numbers, and how much weight they hold.

This closing chapter is different in kind from everything before it. The rest of this course teaches things that can be checked, and it has been unusually strict about that — numbers come from runs, claims get verified before they are written down, and several lessons exist because a check failed. A chapter about the future of work cannot meet that standard, because the future has not happened. What it can do is separate the part that is measured from the part that is argued, and label them.

This lesson is the measured part. The next is the argument.

The aggregate has not moved. The composition has.

The single most important thing in the current evidence is that these two statements are both true, and most commentary picks one and ignores the other.

No aggregate employment effect
National studies, Denmark and the United States
Humlum and Vestergaard, on Danish administrative data across eleven exposed occupations, estimate precise zeros for earnings and hours — confidence intervals ruling out effects larger than one per cent — and find no differential employment change at workplaces that adopted more heavily. The Budget Lab at Yale, tracking the United States economy-wide, reports that measures of AI exposure and usage show no connection to changes in employment or unemployment. If your expectation was mass displacement visible in the headline numbers, it has not arrived.
A sharp compositional effect
Concentrated on the junior end
Hosseini and Lichtinger, on US résumé data covering 62 million workers, find junior employment in adopting firms falling roughly 8 to 10 per cent relative to non-adopters depending on the specification, with senior employment unchanged. Separately, Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen track early-career workers in AI-exposed occupations and report a shortfall against their less-exposed peers that stood at 19 per cent in mid-2026 data, with no comparable gap for experienced workers. Note what the second is and is not: its authors describe these as early descriptive indicators rather than causal estimates, and the headline figure has moved as the data vintage has — it was 16 per cent a year earlier.
Visible in graduate outcomes
US recent-graduate unemployment around 5.6 per cent
The New York Federal Reserve's series for recent graduates — those aged 22 to 27 with a bachelor's degree, not currently enrolled — reads about 5.6 per cent in the second quarter of 2026, up 1.6 points on the same quarter three years earlier. Separately, just over a quarter of employers in a US survey of graduate recruiters report that AI has reduced the need for tasks entry-level workers performed. That survey is small and self-selected at 185 organisations, so treat it as a straw in the wind rather than a measurement.
Slowed hiring rather than firing
The United Kingdom pattern
Firms with high AI exposure have slowed new hiring, particularly for junior positions. That is a different mechanism from redundancy and it is much harder to see, because nothing happens — a role simply is not opened.
Worth knowing

A stable total with a shifting composition is the finding, and it is more interesting than either half.

“AI is destroying jobs” is not supported by the aggregate data. “Nothing is happening” is not supported by the junior-versus-senior split. The evidence describes a labour market of roughly constant size whose entry point is narrowing, and that is a structurally different situation from a shrinking one — with different consequences, on a different timescale, for different people.

For exampleThis is the same reading discipline the evaluation block teaches. An aggregate that has not moved does not mean nothing changed; it means nothing changed on average, and the average is the statistic least able to show you a redistribution.

Productivity: real, modest, and very unevenly distributed

The headline figure
Projections, not measurements
There is no credible measurement of AI-attributable aggregate productivity growth for a single recent year, and figures presented that way should be treated with suspicion. What exists is projection: the OECD puts AI's contribution at 0.25 to 0.6 percentage points of annual total factor productivity growth in the United States over a decade, and Acemoglu's estimate is an order of magnitude smaller at roughly 0.66 per cent in total across ten years. Those are scenarios about the future, they disagree with each other substantially, and neither is a reading of what happened last year.
Concentrated by sector
Knowledge work first, by a wide margin
The OECD's projections put cumulative total factor productivity gains over ten years at about 1 per cent in manual-intensive industries such as agriculture, fishing and mining, against over 10 per cent in knowledge-intensive services like professional and technical services, IT and finance. Cumulative over a decade, not annual, and projected rather than observed — but the direction is the robust part, and it is the one that matters here: the gains land where the work is already language and analysis.
Largest for the least experienced
36 per cent for the lowest-skill quintile
From one study rather than a literature: Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond followed 5,172 customer-support agents through a staggered rollout and measured a 15 per cent average gain in issues resolved per hour, rising to 36 per cent for the lowest-skill quintile. The most skilled saw no significant gain and small declines in resolution quality — so the technology compresses the distribution rather than lifting it uniformly.
And negative past the frontier
19 percentage points less accurate, on one task
Dell'Acqua and colleagues gave 758 consultants a task chosen to sit outside what the model could do. Those using AI were 19 points less likely to reach the correct answer than those without — an average across two conditions that differed a good deal, 13 points and 24. One task, so do not over-read the magnitude; the direction is the point. The tool does not fail quietly at its boundary, it fails confidently, and takes the user with it.
Firms report more gain than their own revenue shows

A consistent finding is that firms report larger productivity gains from AI than their contemporaneous revenue and employment changes imply. The generous reading is a timing lag — output and quality improvements arriving later than the adoption. The ungenerous reading is that self-reported gains from a technology everyone is invested in believing in are exactly the measurement this course would tell you not to trust. Both readings are live, and the data does not yet separate them.

Exposure scores are a proxy, and they miss things

Several figures above are reported for “highly AI-exposed occupations”, and exposure is an estimate of how much of a job's task content a model could plausibly do. It is a reasonable instrument and it is not the thing itself. It cannot see how a particular employer actually organises the work, which parts of a role are tacit, or which tasks turn out to matter more than their share of the hours suggests — and the Shih conversation gives a section to precisely this gap. Treat exposure as a bucket label rather than a measurement of any individual job.

How much weight this evidence holds

Not as much as its precision suggests, and the statistics lesson in the evaluation block is the reason. Many of these figures are single studies of an early deployment, measuring an unstable target during a period of rapid change. A relative decline of 9% and one of 16% are not necessarily in conflict — they are different populations, methods and windows. Treat them as a direction with wide error bars rather than as constants.

Adoption is concentrated where the advantages already were

Adoption is heaviest among large, already-productive firms, and the complementary assets turn out to matter enormously — skilled staff, organisational process, data infrastructure. This is the least surprising and most consequential finding in the set: a technology that multiplies existing capability widens the gap between organisations that had it and those that did not, and the same logic applies to individuals.

Further reading

  • Canaries in the Coal Mine? (Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, Stanford Digital Economy Lab)The early-career employment shortfall, from ADP payroll data. Read the authors' own framing: they call these early descriptive indicators rather than causal estimates, and the headline figure has moved between data vintages.
  • Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change (Hosseini and Lichtinger)US resume and job-posting data on 62 million workers. The junior-versus-senior split, at 8-10 per cent depending on specification. A working paper, not peer-reviewed.
  • Large Language Models, Small Labor Market Effects (Humlum and Vestergaard, NBER 33777)Danish administrative data. Precise zeros on earnings and hours across eleven exposed occupations, with intervals ruling out effects above one per cent.
  • Tracking the Impact of AI on the Labor Market (The Budget Lab at Yale)The US economy-wide picture, updated continuously. Cite the current version rather than a snapshot, because it moves.
  • The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates (Federal Reserve Bank of New York)The graduate unemployment series. Note the definition: aged 22 to 27, bachelor's or higher, not currently enrolled, US only.
  • Generative AI at Work (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, NBER 31161)The customer-support study behind the skill gradient. 5,172 agents, a staggered rollout rather than a randomised experiment. Published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025.
  • Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier (Dell'Acqua et al.)758 consultants, and the task deliberately placed outside the model's reach. The source of the 19-point figure, and of the spread behind it.
  • Macroeconomic productivity gains from AI in G7 economies (OECD, 2025)The sector projections. Cumulative over ten years and modelled rather than observed, which is the part most often dropped when these figures are repeated.
  • The Simple Macroeconomics of AI (Acemoglu, NBER 32487)The considerably more conservative estimate, and worth reading against the OECD's. That two credible estimates differ by an order of magnitude is itself the finding.
