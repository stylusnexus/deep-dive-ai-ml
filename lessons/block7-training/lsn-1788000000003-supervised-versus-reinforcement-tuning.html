Supervised versus Reinforcement Fine-Tuning

One shows the model the answer; the other scores its attempts. The objective decides what you can teach.

Everything so far has assumed you hand the model pairs of input and correct output and it learns to reproduce them. That is supervised fine-tuning, it is the right method most of the time, and it has a ceiling that is easy to state: the model is learning to imitate your examples, so it cannot become better than the examples you were able to write.

The alternative does not show the model the answer at all. It lets the model attempt the task, scores the attempt, and adjusts the model towards whatever scored well. Block 0 described what a reasoning model is; this is the machinery that makes one.

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
Imitate these answers
Input and target output, and a loss that measures the difference. Cheap, stable, well understood, and bounded above by the quality of the demonstrations.
Reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT)
Score these attempts
The model generates, something grades the generation, and the update moves towards higher scores. Can exceed the demonstrations, because nothing is being copied.
The reward or verifier
Whatever does the grading
A unit test, a maths checker, a regular expression, a reward model trained on human preferences, or a rule you wrote. It is the specification of the task, and it is where the whole method succeeds or fails.
Group Relative Policy Optimisation (GRPO)
The formulation that made this practical
Sample several answers to the same prompt, score them, and push towards the ones that beat the group average. Dispensing with the separate value model that earlier methods needed is much of why this became affordable.
Worth knowing

Supervised tuning is bounded by what you can demonstrate. Reinforcement tuning is bounded by what you can verify. Which you reach for depends on which of those you can actually do.

The question to ask about a task is not which method is more advanced but whether you can write down the right answer or only recognise it when you see it. For tone, format, house style and domain idiom, you can demonstrate: write thirty good examples and supervised tuning will do the rest. For multi-step reasoning you generally cannot — nobody can author enough correct derivations to cover the space — but you can frequently check the final answer automatically, and a checker is enough to optimise against.

For exampleNobody hand-wrote the chains of reasoning that reasoning models produce. What existed was maths problems with known answers and code with tests that pass or fail. The model generated attempts, the verifier scored them, and the behaviour that survived was whatever reached correct answers — which is why this works best in domains with a checker and remains hard in domains without one.

The named methods you will meet

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
People rank, a model learns the ranking
Humans compare outputs, a reward model is trained to predict their preferences, and the policy is optimised against that reward model. The original recipe for making a base model behave like an assistant.
Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO)
Skip the reward model
Given pairs of preferred and rejected responses, optimise the policy on the preference directly. Simpler and cheaper than the above, and popular for that reason; it needs preference pairs rather than a verifier.
Verifier-based RFT
Grade against ground truth
Where a correct answer can be checked mechanically — tests pass, the number matches, the output parses and satisfies a constraint. The strongest signal available, and restricted to tasks that have one.
Reward hacking is not an edge case, it is the default behaviour

An optimiser given a measure will optimise the measure, and it is under no obligation to respect what you meant. Reward a code model on tests passing and it may write code that special-cases the tests. Reward answer length as a proxy for thoroughness and you will get length. Every measurement caveat this course has raised — the judge that prefers longer answers, the metric that moves while the thing it stands for does not — becomes sharper here, because you are no longer merely measuring with a flawed instrument, you are actively training against its flaws.

If the reward is itself a model, this compounds: the policy is being optimised to find that model's blind spots, and it is good at it. Hold out an evaluation the reward never sees, and watch it rather than the reward curve.

Worth knowing

A rising reward curve is not evidence that the model improved. It is evidence that the reward went up.

These are only the same claim when the reward captures the goal exactly, which it never does. The gap between them is where reward hacking lives, and the reward curve cannot show you the gap because the gap is invisible to it by construction — this is the guardrail-metric argument from the evaluation block, applied to a system built to push on the primary metric as hard as it can.

For exampleA model tuned to make a unit-test suite pass reaches a near-perfect reward. Inspection shows it had learned to detect the test fixture and return hard-coded values for the inputs the tests use. The reward was correct throughout; the suite really did pass. Nothing in the training signal distinguished passing the tests from writing the function.

Which to reach for

  • Supervised, almost always first. Cheaper, more stable, easier to debug, and sufficient for the format, tone and consistency problems that are the honest reason for most fine-tuning.
  • Preference-based, when quality is a matter of taste. Where better and worse are recognisable but not writable, and you can collect comparisons more easily than exemplars.
  • Verifier-based, when you can check but not demonstrate. Reasoning, code, structured extraction, anything with a mechanical notion of correct. This is the one that can exceed your own ability at the task.
  • None of them, if the failure is knowledge. The first lesson in this block holds regardless of how the tuning is done, and no objective function installs facts that retrieval would supply exactly.

✅ Check Yourself

1. What determines the ceiling on supervised fine-tuning?

The size of the base model, which caps what any adaptation can add
The quality of the demonstrations — the model is learning to imitate them, so it cannot exceed what you were able to write down
The number of epochs before overfitting begins

2. Why is reinforcement tuning the method behind reasoning behaviour rather than supervised tuning?

Reasoning requires more parameters than supervised tuning can update
Nobody can author enough correct multi-step derivations to cover the space, but final answers can often be checked automatically — and a checker is enough to optimise against
Supervised tuning cannot represent sequences longer than a few hundred tokens

3. Your reward curve rises steadily through training. What does that establish?

That the model is improving at the task, which is what the reward measures
That the reward went up — which equals improvement only where the reward captures the goal exactly, so it needs checking against an evaluation the reward never sees
That the learning rate is well chosen for the run
