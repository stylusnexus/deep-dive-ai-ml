Lesson 2 of 6

Building the Dataset, and How Little You Need

The part that decides whether the training run was worth doing — and it is smaller than people expect.

Assume the previous lesson went badly for you and fine-tuning really is the answer. The dataset is now the whole project. Everything downstream — the method, the rank, the hardware, the hours — is mechanical by comparison, and none of it recovers from examples that disagree with each other.

The number is smaller than you think

The intuition people bring from classical machine learning is that more rows are better, and it is wrong here. A base model already speaks the language, knows the domain vocabulary and can follow a format; you are not teaching it to write, you are showing it which of the things it can already do you want. Low-rank adaptation on a narrow, consistent task frequently works on several hundred examples, and a few thousand is a large dataset rather than a small one.

What replaces volume is consistency and coverage. Five hundred examples that all handle the same situation the same way will teach a behaviour. Fifty thousand that disagree with each other will teach the disagreement, and will do it very thoroughly.

Consistency
The property that actually matters
Two examples handling the same case differently do not average out. They teach the model that both are acceptable, which is exactly what you were fine-tuning to stop.
Coverage
Span the cases, not the volume
Better to have twenty examples across ten situations than two hundred across two. The model generalises from the shape of what it saw, and it cannot generalise to a case that has no representative.
Format fidelity
Train the way you will serve
The chat template, the system prompt, the field names and the whitespace all have to match what production sends. A model tuned on a slightly different envelope is being asked to generalise across the one difference you controlled and got wrong.
The held-out split
Made first, opened last
Split before you look at anything, and do not iterate against it. A split carved out after you have been reading the data has already been contaminated by your own judgement.
Worth knowing

A dataset teaches the whole distribution it contains, including the parts you did not intend to teach.

The model has no way to know which properties of your examples were the point. If ninety-five per cent of them are one category, you have taught a prior on that category. If every single one is a confident, complete answer, you have taught it that confident and complete is always available — and quietly removed its willingness to say the thing is not in the document, which is the behaviour the retrieval and structured-output lessons both spent their time protecting.

For exampleA team fine-tunes a support classifier on eight hundred resolved tickets. Resolved tickets, by definition, are the ones that had an answer. The tuned model stops routing anything to a human, because in eight hundred examples it never once saw that outcome, and its accuracy on the eval set — also drawn from resolved tickets — looks excellent.

Where the examples come from

Production traffic is the best source and the most work: it carries the distribution you will actually serve, including the awkward inputs nobody would think to invent, and it arrives unlabelled. Hand-written examples are the highest quality per row and do not scale past a few hundred, which is fine, because a few hundred is often the whole requirement. Generating examples with a larger model is cheap and is where the traps live.

Two traps in model-generated data, one legal and one technical

Check the licence on the model whose outputs you are training on. Several providers prohibit using their outputs to train competing models, and a dataset built in breach of that is a dataset you cannot ship, which you would rather learn before the training run than after.

The technical trap is subtler and is the point of the loops-in-training lesson: a generated dataset inherits the generator's distribution, including its errors and its blind spots, and it under-represents exactly the rare cases that were the reason you were fine-tuning. Generated data is reasonable for teaching format, which the generator gets right by construction. It is a poor way to teach judgement, which is the thing it may not have.

Worth knowing

Deduplicate across the split, or your evaluation will tell you the training worked.

Near-duplicates are ordinary in real corpora — the same ticket refiled, the same document in two formats, boilerplate repeated across records. If one copy lands in training and another in the held-out set, the model has seen the answer, and the score you read afterwards is measuring memorisation. This is data leakage from Block 1, arriving in a form the usual split-before-you-fit discipline does not catch, because the split was done correctly and the duplication was already in the data.

For exampleA support dataset of twelve hundred tickets contains roughly ninety near-identical password-reset requests. Split at random, the held-out set gets about eighteen of them and the model has trained on the rest. Accuracy comes back four points higher than the model deserves, and every one of those points is a ticket it had already been shown.

What good looks like before you start

  • The evaluation set exists, was split first, and nothing has been tuned against it.
  • You can state what a wrong answer looks like, and there are examples of the model getting it right in that specific way.
  • The hard cases and the ones where the correct answer is a refusal are both represented, in something like the proportion you expect to serve.
  • Two people reading a sample of the data agree on what the label should have been. If they do not, the model will not either.
  • The format is byte-for-byte what production sends, chat template included.

✅ Check Yourself

1. Why can five hundred consistent examples outperform fifty thousand inconsistent ones?

Smaller datasets overfit less, which improves generalisation
Contradictory examples teach that both behaviours are acceptable, which is the behaviour fine-tuning was meant to remove — consistency is the signal, volume is not
Large datasets require a higher rank, which costs more memory

2. Every example in a support dataset is a ticket that was successfully resolved. What has the model been taught?

Nothing problematic — resolved tickets are the correct behaviour to imitate
That an answer is always available, because it never saw the outcome where the right move was to escalate — and the eval set, drawn from the same pool, cannot detect it
To prefer shorter answers, since resolutions tend to be brief

3. A near-duplicate record appears in both the training and held-out sets. What does the evaluation measure?

Generalisation, since the records are not byte-identical
Partly memorisation — the model has effectively seen the held-out answer, so the score is inflated by an amount you cannot see
Nothing is affected as long as the split was random
