The Four Ways to Shrink a Model

Quantization, pruning, distillation and factorisation — what each removes, and which of them compose.

Block 0 taught quantization and distillation separately, in the places they came up. They are two members of a family of four, and seeing the family is what tells you which to reach for, because they remove different things and only some of them combine.

Quantization
Fewer bits per number
The same architecture, the same parameter count, stored less precisely. Cheapest by a wide margin, needs no training, and is reversible in the sense that the original weights still exist. Usually the first and often the only thing needed.
Pruning
Fewer parts
Remove structure from the trained network. Depth pruning takes whole layers; width pruning takes neurons and attention heads within layers. Unlike quantization this changes the architecture, and the model is worse immediately afterwards.
Distillation
A smaller model taught to imitate
Train a student to reproduce a teacher's behaviour. The one that produces a genuinely different model rather than a modified one, and the most expensive of the four.
Low-rank factorisation
The same layer, expressed with fewer numbers
A large weight matrix approximated as the product of two smaller ones. Least used of the four at inference time and conceptually the most familiar, because it is what LoRA already does — an adapter is a low-rank pair standing in for a full-rank update.

Pruning and distillation are the pair that compose

The other three are alternatives; these two are a sequence. Pruning removes structure and the model loses capability with it — the cut is blunt, and what falls out is whatever those layers or heads were contributing. Distillation is then used to repair, training the pruned model against the original as teacher so it recovers behaviour it just lost. Cut, then heal. This is the one place in the family where two techniques are used together rather than chosen between, and it is why a pruning pipeline is more work than the word suggests.

Worth knowing

Compression does not remove capability evenly, and the loss is not visible in an average.

A pruned or quantized model that scores within a point of the original on a general benchmark has not necessarily kept everything. Capability is not distributed uniformly across a network, so cutting parts of it removes some things and leaves others — and an aggregate score can absorb a large loss in a narrow area without moving. What disappears tends to be what was rare in the first place: less-represented languages, minority dialects, uncommon domains, the long tail generally.

For exampleThis is why compression is a fairness question and not only an efficiency one. If the capability lost falls disproportionately on the users who were already least well served, an average that barely moved has hidden exactly the harm worth knowing about. Evaluate compressed models on slices, not on a single number.

Slice the evaluation before and after

The measurement discipline from Block 5 applies directly and is usually skipped here. Record the baseline per slice — per language, per domain, per user population you can identify — and compare per slice afterwards. A single aggregate is precisely the instrument that cannot detect uneven loss, and reporting one is how a compressed model ships having quietly become worse for a subset of the people using it.

Quantization is the boring answer and usually the right one

It requires no training run, no dataset, no student model and no repair pass, and on most serving constraints it is sufficient on its own. Pruning and distillation pipelines are more interesting to build and considerably more work to maintain — a variant you now own, re-derived on every base-model upgrade. Reach for them when quantization has been tried and has not met the constraint, rather than because they are the more sophisticated option.

Where each one bites

Choose by the constraint you actually have. If the model will not fit in memory, quantization addresses it directly and cheaply. If it fits but generation is too slow, remember from Block 0 that generation is memory-bound — so fewer bytes helps and fewer operations does not, which favours quantization again over anything that only removes arithmetic. If you need a genuinely smaller model for a narrow task, distillation is the honest answer. Pruning sits between: real gains, real cost, and a repair pass attached.
