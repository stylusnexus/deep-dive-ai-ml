Lesson 1 of 6

When Fine-Tuning Is the Wrong Answer

Which is most of the time, and the order to exhaust before you reach for it.

Fine-tuning is the intervention people reach for first and should reach for last. It is the only one that changes the model, which makes it the only one that cannot be undone by editing a file, the only one that has to be redone when the base model improves, and the only one whose failures are invisible until evaluation. This lesson is the order to work through before you get there.

The ladder

Prompting
Costs tokens, changes nothing permanent
Reversible in a text editor, and it improves when the model does. Exhaust it properly — few-shot examples, output constraints, an explicit format — before concluding the model cannot do the task.
Retrieval
Gives the model facts it never had
If the failure is that the model does not know something, no amount of fine-tuning on your task format will fix it, and retrieval will. Confusing these two is the commonest expensive mistake in this area.
Fine-tuning
Teaches form, not facts
Changes how the model responds — tone, structure, adherence to a schema, a domain's conventions. It is poor at installing knowledge and good at installing behaviour, and the distinction decides whether it is the right tool.
Structural change
Pruning, distillation, quantization
A different axis entirely: not what the model does but how much of it there is. Covered in the next lesson, and usually driven by a serving constraint rather than a quality one.
Worth knowing

Fine-tuning teaches form. Retrieval supplies facts. Most projects that reach for the first needed the second.

A model that answers in the wrong register, ignores your schema or misses your domain's conventions has a form problem and fine-tuning addresses it directly. A model that does not know your product names, your policies or last quarter's numbers has a knowledge problem, and training on documents containing them is an expensive and unreliable way to install facts that a retrieval index would hold exactly and update in seconds.

For exampleThe tell is what a wrong answer looks like. If it is confidently wrong about a fact, you have a retrieval problem. If it is right but formatted, toned or structured wrongly, you have a fine-tuning problem — and only the second one is worth a training run.

Every base-model upgrade repeats the work

A fine-tuned model is a fork. The moment a better base appears, your adapter was trained against the old one and the choice is to retrain or to stay behind. Over a year of frequent releases that is a recurring cost nobody budgets at the point of deciding, and it is the strongest practical argument for exhausting the reversible options first.
