Is the Difference Real?

Pairing, spread, Bessel's correction and the peeking problem — the arithmetic that decides whether a number moved or wobbled.

Block 2 says two models whose confidence intervals overlap are not distinguishable however different their means look. Nothing carried that into this block, so every score here has been read as though it were exact. This lesson is the repair, and its numbers come from simulations in scripts/measure_eval_statistics.py rather than from assertion.

What each of these costs you when you do not know it

This is the only lesson in the course that looks like statistics, so it is worth being blunt about why it earns its place. Each item below has a failure attached, and every one of them is a failure that looks like success at the time.

Not knowing about pairing
You abandon improvements that worked
You make a real change, test it wrongly, see nothing, and conclude the idea failed. The simulation later in this lesson takes a genuine effect from detected almost always to detected almost never purely by testing it the wrong way. The work was fine; the verdict was an artefact.
Reporting only means
You ship a change that broke a subset
The average moved up, so it looked good. Half your users got worse and nothing in the report said so, and you find out from them rather than from the measurement.
Dividing by n
You are most overconfident exactly when you should be least
It understates spread, and the error is largest at small sample sizes — which is every eval set in this course. Overstated confidence on ten questions is how a coin-flip difference gets written into a document as a finding.
Peeking at a running test
You accumulate wins that never add up
Each individual result looked significant when you stopped. None of them were, so none of them compound, and a year later the product is not better and nobody can say which of the twenty improvements failed to arrive.

None of this requires becoming a statistician. It requires knowing that a number produced by a measurement has a width, and four specific ways that width gets misread.

Pairing, which is the one that matters most

Two measurements are paired when each one in the first set has a natural partner in the second — the same unit, measured twice. An eval is paired by construction: question one is scored under system A and under system B, and those two numbers are about the same question. An A/B test on live traffic is not paired, because different users were assigned to different arms and no user appears in both.

The distinction decides which test to run, and the cost of getting it wrong is larger than it sounds. Question difficulty varies enormously — some questions are hard for everything and some are easy for everything — and in a paired comparison that difficulty affects both members of each pair equally, so subtracting one from the other removes it. An unpaired test cannot subtract it and leaves it in the noise, where it usually dwarfs the effect you are looking for.

20 questions, a real effect of +0.30, difficulty varying by ~1.0

  unpaired test detects it :   0.6% of the time
  paired   test detects it :  95.9% of the time
Worth knowing

The unpaired test is not wrong. It is answering a question nobody asked.

It asks whether two unrelated piles of numbers have different means. The question you actually had was whether B beats A question by question, and the pairing is the information that turns one into the other. Throwing it away is not a conservative choice or a safe default — in the simulation above it took a real, large effect from almost always detected to almost never, on identical data.

For exampleThe rule is simple: if you can line the two sets up one-to-one and the pairing means something, use a paired test. Same questions through two models, same code before and after a change, same document scored by two judges.

Real is not the same as large

Everything above establishes whether a difference is real — whether it is more than noise. That is a different question from whether it is big enough to act on, and the two get run together constantly because significance is the number people report.

The distinction has a direction that matters: a large enough sample makes almost any difference significant, including differences far too small to change a decision. Run enough items and a 0.2 percentage point improvement becomes unambiguously real, and it is still 0.2 percentage points. The size of the difference is its effect size, and it is the half that tells you whether to ship.

Worth knowing

Report the size alongside the significance, because significance alone cannot tell a discovery from a rounding error.

A p-value answers “is this probably not noise?” and nothing else. It says nothing about magnitude, and it gets easier to satisfy as the sample grows — so a team that reports only significance will, given enough data, keep finding real and worthless results. The two failure modes are symmetrical and both are common: acting on a difference that was noise, and acting on a difference that was real and negligible.

For exampleTwo prompt variants are compared over 4,000 items. B beats A by 0.3 percentage points, comfortably significant at that sample size. It is also 0.3 percentage points, against a change that doubles the prompt length and therefore the cost of every call forever. The result is real and the decision is still no.

Two intervals that get confused, and only one is about your next case

An interval around a result can answer two different questions and the notation does not distinguish them. A confidence interval is about the average: run the experiment many times and the true mean lies inside it as often as the confidence level says. A prediction interval is about a single new observation: where the next case is likely to land. The second is always wider, often much wider, because it carries both the uncertainty in the estimate and the ordinary spread of the data.

Worth knowing

A confidence interval on the mean tells you nothing about how wrong you can be on one case.

Anything making a decision per item — a per-request latency budget, a per-customer forecast, a per-document score threshold — is asking a prediction question, and a confidence interval answers a different one. The gap between them is not a technicality: as the sample grows, the confidence interval shrinks towards nothing while the prediction interval converges on the actual spread of the data and stays wide, because knowing the mean perfectly still does not tell you what the next draw will be.

For exampleA model's mean response time is 400ms with a 95 per cent confidence interval of 390 to 410. It would be easy to promise a 500ms budget on that basis. The prediction interval is 180 to 1,300, because individual requests vary enormously, and roughly one request in twenty will breach the promise. The confidence interval was never evidence about individual requests.

Report the distribution per arm, not just the mean

A mean is one number standing in for a shape, and two arms with identical means can behave completely differently. The one that catches people is bimodality: a change that helps half the population and hurts the other half moves the mean not at all, and the summary reports success by reporting nothing.

Per-arm spread
The minimum honest report
Mean and standard deviation for each arm separately, not a single difference. Two arms whose distributions overlap heavily are not distinguishable however far apart the means sit.
The shape, not only the width
Where bimodality hides
Look at the distribution. A change that improved most cases slightly and broke a few badly has a mean that moved up and a tail that should stop the release.
Slices as arms
The connection to Block 7
Uneven capability loss under compression is this same problem: an aggregate that barely moves while one population degrades. Per-arm distribution and per-slice evaluation are the same instrument pointed at different questions.

Bessel's correction, and when it matters

Sample variance divides by n − 1 rather than n, and the reason is that the mean was fitted to the same data. The sample mean is by definition the value that makes the squared deviations smallest, so deviations measured from it are systematically smaller than deviations from the true mean would be. Dividing by n inherits that and underestimates the spread. Subtracting one accounts for the single degree of freedom spent estimating the mean.

true variance is 1.000 by construction

      n           divide by n         divide by n-1
      5        0.796 (-20.4%)        0.995 ( -0.5%)
     10        0.897 (-10.3%)        0.997 ( -0.3%)
     30        0.968 ( -3.2%)        1.002 ( +0.2%)
    100        0.989 ( -1.1%)        0.999 ( -0.1%)
   1000        0.999 ( -0.1%)        1.000 ( +0.0%)
The same ceiling fails in both directions, and both are silent

The evaluation material here treats a low output ceiling as the hazard: a reasoning model spends the budget thinking, returns an empty string, and scores as wrong. The mirror is just as real. Set the ceiling high and a turn that degenerates into repeating itself will consume the entire budget before stopping — in one observed case, 31,999 tokens of a 32,000 limit and forty-five minutes of wall clock, producing nothing, twice in a row.

So “raise it” is not the safe default and neither is “lower it”. The ceiling bounds the damage from a loop that has already gone wrong; it does not prevent the loop, and choosing it well means knowing which of the two failures you are currently exposed to.

It matters at eval sizes and nowhere else

At a thousand samples the correction is invisible and arguing about it is pedantry. At ten — which is the size of the eval sets in this course — dividing by n understates the variance by a tenth, and understating variance means overstating confidence in exactly the situation where you have least of it. The correction is free, so the habit is simply to use it and stop thinking about it.

A/B testing, and the peek that ruins it

An A/B test assigns real traffic randomly between arms and compares outcomes. It answers a question an offline eval cannot — how the change behaves for actual users on actual requests — and it comes with a discipline the offline version does not need, because the data arrives continuously and you can watch it.

Random assignment
What makes the arms comparable
Assign by a stable identifier so a user stays in one arm; assigning per request means the same person experiences both and the comparison is neither paired nor independent.
Sample size decided in advance
Before any data arrives
The measurement procedure already says write the number down before you run. Here it is load-bearing: without a pre-declared stopping point there is no such thing as a significant result.
One primary metric, plus guardrails
Decided beforehand
One metric decides the call. Guardrail metrics are the things that must not get worse — latency, cost, refusal rate — and they are checked but do not get to declare victory.
Peeking
The failure that manufactures results
Checking repeatedly and stopping when it looks significant. Every look is another chance for noise to cross the line, and noise eventually will.

Worth knowing

Peeking turned a five percent error rate into nearly twenty, on data with no effect in it at all.

The nominal rate is what you agreed to accept when you chose the threshold. Stopping the moment a running experiment crosses it converts a single test into ten, and the chance that at least one of ten crosses by luck is far higher than the chance that one does. Nothing about the arithmetic of the individual test is wrong; the error is in how many times it was consulted.

For exampleThe first line of that output is also the instrument check. A test that did not land near five percent on data with no effect would have told me the implementation was broken before it told me anything about peeking.

A t-test is the wrong tool for a proportion

Most of this course's headline numbers are proportions — six of nine correct, twenty-four of twenty-four flagged. A t-test assumes roughly normal continuous measurements and binary outcomes at small n are neither. Use a test built for proportions, or a bootstrap, and be aware that at nine items almost nothing will be significant. Project 2 moving from five of nine to six of nine is a direction, not a result, and this course said so at the time on judgement rather than on arithmetic.
