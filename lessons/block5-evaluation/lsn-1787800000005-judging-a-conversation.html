Lesson 6 of 6

Judging a Conversation

Every score so far graded one answer. An agent produces a trajectory, and the failure is usually three turns back.

Everything this block has measured is a single response to a single question: was it grounded, was it correct, did it refuse when it should. Block 4 builds systems that do not work that way. An agent takes a sequence of turns, each one conditioned on everything before it, and grading the last message tells you almost nothing about what happened.

Why the last turn is the wrong unit

A final answer can be right after a run that was expensive, circular and lucky, or wrong after a run that reasoned correctly and hit a tool that was down. Scoring only the ending scores the outcome and not the behaviour, and the behaviour is the thing you can improve.

The failure has a location
And it is rarely the last turn
A wrong final answer usually traces to a specific earlier step: a retrieval that returned nothing useful, a tool result misread, a plan abandoned. Grading the ending records that something went wrong and discards where.
Right for the wrong reasons
The score a single turn cannot catch
An agent that guessed, or that recovered from its own mistake by luck, produces the same final answer as one that worked correctly. On a single-turn metric they are indistinguishable, and only one of them will keep working.
Recovery is a property worth scoring
Not just success
Agents will hit failures. What separates a usable one is whether it notices and adapts. A trajectory that failed a tool call, read the error and tried something sensible is better than one that succeeded first time by luck, and a final-answer score ranks them the other way round.
Cost is part of the outcome
Turns, tokens and calls
Two runs reaching the same answer in three turns and in eleven are not equally good. This is the one dimension people do remember to record and then leave out of the judgement.
Worth knowing

Score the trajectory, and the final answer is one dimension of it rather than the whole grade.

The trace from Block 4 already contains what you need — every turn, every tool call, every result, the termination reason. Judging it as a sequence turns a pass or fail into a diagnosis, and the diagnosis is what tells you which of the loop, the tools, the prompt or the retrieval to fix. A single-turn score gives you a number that moves without telling you why, which is the position this course spent Block 5 arguing against.

For exampleThis is the same argument as measuring index recall, retrieval quality and groundedness separately rather than as one accuracy number. Separate the dimensions that fail independently, because they have different fixes.

What to score, and by what

Deterministically, wherever possible
Turn count, tool calls, cost, termination reason, guards fired
None of this needs a judge. It is in the trace already, it is exact, and it catches a large share of what goes wrong. Exhaust it before reaching for a model, exactly as the grading ladder says.
Per-step, against the step's own job
Did this retrieval return the right document?
Each step has a checkable purpose. Scoring steps individually localises the failure, which is the whole point of doing this rather than grading the ending.
The trajectory as a whole
Where a judge earns its place
Was the sequence sensible, did it recover, did it go in circles? This is the genuinely open-ended part and the one worth a rubric — and the rubric lesson's rules apply unchanged, including blinding.
A judge shown a whole trajectory will grade the ending

Hand a model a full transcript and ask how the run went, and it anchors on the final answer — a good ending makes the path look purposeful in hindsight. If you want the path judged, ask about the path specifically and separately: was any turn wasted, was the tool result read correctly, was the plan abandoned without reason. One question per property, or you get a summary of the outcome wearing a rubric.

Multi-turn state is where the eval set gets expensive

A single-turn set is a list of questions. A multi-turn set is a list of situations — a starting state, the turns so far, and often a scripted counterpart to play the other side. That is considerably more work to build and to keep correct, which is why most teams claim to test agents and actually test the last message. Start with a handful of realistic trajectories rather than a large set of shallow ones.

Where this connects

Block 4's telemetry lesson insisted the trace falls out of the loop for a few lines of code, and this is what it was for. The trace is not only for debugging an incident — it is the object you evaluate. A system with traces and no trajectory evaluation is recording the evidence and never reading it.
