The Eval Battery

How you know any of this works — and why the number you trust must be one you built.

A serious agent project accumulates a bench/ directory, a record of past runs, and a benchmarks document. That machinery is what makes any claim about the system checkable, and it is the bridge into Block 5.

A battery is a fixed set of tasks with checkable outcomes

Each task has an input, a way to run it, and a way to decide pass or fail without a human. Run the battery against a configuration and you get a score. Change one thing, re-run, compare. That's the entire method, and its value comes from the set staying fixed.

A recorded result — say 11 of 24 on multi-file editing as of a particular change — is only meaningful because the 24 did not move. A benchmark you edit between runs measures nothing.

Battery, eval harness, agent harness — three thingsA battery is the set of tasks. An eval harness is the machinery that runs them and produces a score. An agent harness is the scaffolding around a single agent run — prompt assembly, tools, turn limits — which is the sense the loop lesson uses. The exam analogy: the battery is the question paper and the mark scheme; the eval harness is the invigilator and the process that totals the marks; the agent harness is how one candidate sits the exam. Context disambiguates, and the field genuinely uses the word both ways.
The battery is not the productAn eval harness frequently builds its own configuration rather than the one your real system runs — a different system prompt, different budgets, sometimes no whole-run clock at all. When that is true, a result from the battery is evidence about the harness that ran it, not about the thing you ship. Check whether the two configurations actually match before treating a board of numbers as a statement about your product.

Why public benchmarks won't answer your question

  • Contamination. Popular benchmarks are in the training data. High scores may be recall, not capability.
  • Optimisation pressure. Vendors tune for benchmarks that decide purchases. The measure stops being a measure.
  • Task mismatch. A model's competitive-programming score says little about editing four files in your Go codebase without breaking the build.
The twenty-item ruleThe single most valuable evaluation asset you can own is about twenty real tasks from your actual work, with known-good outcomes. It's small enough to build in an afternoon and specific enough that no vendor has optimised for it. Block 5 builds yours; HF Inference Providers then runs it across many models on one bill.

Grading, from cheapest to loosest

Deterministic
Compiles, tests pass, schema validates
Cheap, unambiguous, trustworthy. Use wherever possible.
Reference match
Compare against a known-good answer
Exact match, or fuzzy for prose. Brittle when many answers are valid.
Model-as-judge
Another model scores the output
Flexible, and the last resort — it inherits the judge's biases and can be circular if the judge is the model under test.
The trap the harness is named forAn agent asked whether it succeeded will frequently say yes. The strongest eval harnesses are built on exactly this premise — replay real closed pull requests, so the outcome is established in advance and the agent's self-report is irrelevant. Never let the thing being evaluated report its own score.

What to record on every run

A score alone is nearly useless six weeks later. Record the model and quantization, the harness version or commit, the sampling settings, the date, and the per-task outcomes rather than only the total. Without those, a changed number tells you something moved but not what.

Worth knowing

Twenty real tasks from your own work, with known-good outcomes, is the most valuable evaluation asset you can own.

It is small enough to build in an afternoon and specific enough that no vendor has optimised against it. Public benchmarks are contaminated, gamed, and usually measuring a different problem from yours.

For exampleA model tops the leaderboard on a coding benchmark and performs poorly on your Go service. Both facts are true. The benchmark is Python, drawn from public repositories that were in the training data. Your twenty tasks are your codebase, your conventions, and your idea of a correct answer — which is the thing you actually need to predict.
