Class Imbalance and Choosing a Threshold

Why 99% accuracy can mean a model that has learned nothing at all.

One transaction in a thousand is fraudulent. A model that predicts "not fraud" every single time is 99.9% accurate and completely worthless. This is class imbalance, and it makes the most intuitive metric in machine learning actively misleading.

Accuracy breaks first

Accuracy is correct predictions over total predictions. When one class dominates, the majority-class guess sets a very high floor, and beating that floor by a little can look like a strong result while representing no useful skill at all.

Always compute the trivial baselineBefore reporting any accuracy figure, work out what 'always guess the majority class' scores. If your model is at 99.2% and the trivial baseline is 99.1%, you have not built anything. Your intake sheet asks for the dumb baseline for exactly this reason — on imbalanced data it isn't a formality, it's the whole interpretation.

Use precision and recall instead

Your statistics course covers these in depth. The short version: precision asks what fraction of the things you flagged were real; recall asks what fraction of the real things you caught. Neither can hide behind a dominant negative class, which is why both are reported on imbalanced problems and accuracy usually isn't.

The threshold is a decision, not a default

A classifier outputs a probability. Turning it into an action requires a cut-off, and 0.5 is a convention inherited from balanced problems — nothing more. Moving the threshold trades the two error types against each other directly:

Which end you want is question 6 on the intake sheet. Screening for a treatable disease: recall matters far more, because a missed case is catastrophic and a false alarm costs one further test. Auto-blocking customer accounts: precision matters more, because wrongly locking out real customers is a serious harm and a missed case can be caught later.

Handling imbalance during training

Class weights
Penalise minority errors more
One parameter in most libraries. Try this first — it's nearly free.
Oversampling / SMOTE
Duplicate or synthesise minority rows
Fit only on the training split. Doing it before the split is textbook leakage.
Undersampling
Discard majority rows
Fast, throws away real data. Reasonable when the majority class is enormous.
Threshold tuning
Change nothing, move the cut-off
Frequently the most effective option, and the most often skipped.
The leakage trap hereOversample before splitting and copies of the same minority row land in both train and test. Your model has effectively seen the test data, the score is inflated, and the usual overfitting gap won't show it. This is one of the most common leakage mistakes in practice — resample inside the pipeline, after the split, every time.

Where this reappears

Every rare-event problem in the program: fraud, equipment failure, safety incidents, and the retrieval evaluations in Block 5 where the relevant documents are a tiny fraction of the corpus. The reflex worth building is to check the class balance before reading any metric at all.

Precision — avoid alarming people unnecessarily
Recall — catch as many real cases as possible, accepting more false alarms
