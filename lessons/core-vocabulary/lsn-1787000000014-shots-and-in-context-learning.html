Zero-shot, Few-shot and In-Context Learning

Teaching a model a task in the prompt — what it is, and where it stops working.

You can change what a model does without changing a single weight, by putting examples in the prompt. This is in-context learning, and it's the reason prompt engineering became a discipline rather than a footnote.

Counting shots

Zero-shot
Instruction, no examples
"Classify the sentiment of this review." Works well for tasks the model has seen many times in training.
One-shot
A single example
Often enough to pin down output format, which is frequently the actual problem.
Few-shot
Typically 2–10 examples
The workhorse. Demonstrates format, edge-case handling, and tone in one move.
Many-shot
Dozens to hundreds
Now viable with long contexts. Closes some of the gap to fine-tuning, at the cost of paying for those tokens on every single call.

The word 'learning' is doing something unusual here

Nothing is learned in the training sense. No weight changes, and the model retains nothing after the call. The examples condition the model's next-token distribution for that request only — closer to demonstration than instruction, and completely forgotten the moment the context is gone.

Why this matters practicallyFew-shot examples cost tokens on every request, forever. Ten good examples might be 800 tokens. At a million calls that's 800 million tokens you pay for repeatedly — the point at which fine-tuning stops looking expensive. This is the calculation Block 7 opens with.

What examples are actually good at

The counter-intuitive finding from the research: for many tasks, the format and label space of your examples matter more than whether the labels are correct. Examples show the model what a valid answer looks like — the shape, the vocabulary, the granularity. That's why they fix formatting problems so reliably and reasoning problems so unreliably.

  • Excellent for: output format, tone, label vocabulary, granularity, handling edge cases you name.
  • Weak for: teaching genuinely new knowledge, or complex multi-step reasoning the model can't already do.

How to choose examples

  • Cover the classes. Five positive examples and one negative teaches a bias toward positive.
  • Include the awkward cases. The ambiguous review, the empty field, the one that should be refused. Those are what you're actually buying.
  • Order matters. Models show recency bias toward later examples. Vary the order when testing rather than assuming stability.
  • Keep them consistent. Examples that contradict each other in format produce output that contradicts itself.
  • Retrieve them dynamically. Pick examples similar to the current input rather than using a fixed set — few-shot selection as a retrieval problem, and often a significant gain.
The evaluation trapTuning your examples against the same handful of test cases you evaluate on is leakage by hand. The prompt gets excellent at those five inputs and no better at anything else. Hold examples back, exactly as you would rows of data.

When to stop adding examples and fine-tune instead

Few-shot stops being the right answer when: the examples no longer fit comfortably in context; you're paying for the same tokens at high volume; the task needs knowledge the model doesn't have rather than a format it hasn't seen; or you've plateaued and adding examples no longer moves your eval. Block 7 covers what to do then — and opens by arguing you usually shouldn't.
