Distillation

Training a small model to imitate a large one — and getting most of the capability at a fraction of the cost.

You have a large model that works and a bill that doesn't. Distillation trains a small "student" model to reproduce a large "teacher" model's behaviour. Done well, you keep most of the capability at a fraction of the size, latency and cost.

Why it works better than just training a small model

Train a small model on hard labels and each example teaches it one thing: this is a cat. Train it on the teacher's full output distribution and the same example teaches it far more — this is a cat, it's somewhat dog-like, it's nothing like a car. Those relative probabilities carry information about how the teacher organises the problem, and that's a richer signal than the label alone.

The name for that extra signalIt's often called dark knowledge — the information in the probabilities the teacher assigns to wrong answers. A small model trained on it reliably outperforms the same architecture trained on labels alone. You are transferring how the teacher thinks, not just what it concludes.

The forms you'll meet

Response distillation
Student copies final outputs
Generate answers with the teacher, fine-tune the student on them. Simplest, and what most people mean by distillation now.
Logit distillation
Student matches the full distribution
The classical form. Needs access to the teacher's raw outputs, so it's mostly for open-weight teachers.
Rationale distillation
Student copies the reasoning too
Teacher produces chain-of-thought; student learns to reproduce it. Effective on reasoning-heavy tasks.
Task-specific distillation
One narrow job
Where the wins are largest — a 7B student can match a frontier teacher on one well-defined task while being useless outside it.
Context distillation
Teacher and student are the same model
The only form where nothing is transferred between two different models. The teacher is the model with a long document sitting in its context; the student is that same model with a small trained cache standing in for the document. Both see the same question, and the student learns to answer as though it had read something it never sees at inference.

The objective decides what you get

Context distillation is worth dwelling on because it comes with a result that generalises well past it. Suppose you want a small cache that behaves as though a corpus were present. The obvious way to train it is next-token prediction over that corpus — the objective language models are trained on in the first place, applied to exactly the text you care about.

It does not work. Researchers report it as not competitive with simply putting the corpus in the context, and the reason is not a tuning failure. Next-token prediction teaches the model to continue the document. A cache that has to serve questions needs to answer arbitrary things about the document, which is a different skill. Same corpus, same artefact, same size, and the objective alone decides whether you get something useful.

Worth knowing

Where the training starts decides where it ends, and the gap is not small.

The same work reports a trained cache initialised from random noise reaching 29.9% on a long-context benchmark, against 55.3% for one initialised from the model's real cache of the first stretch of the corpus. Identical size, identical recipe, identical data — only the starting point differed, and it nearly doubled the score. Initialisation is usually filed under implementation detail; here it is the difference between a working artefact and a broken one.

For exampleThe practical form of this: when a training run underperforms and the recipe looks right, check what it started from before you change the recipe.

The training data can come from the corpus itself

If a cache is trained on questions, and nobody has asked any yet, where do the questions come from? One published recipe has the model hold a conversation with itself about each chunk in turn, playing both the asking and the answering side, steered by a handful of generic prompts that say nothing about any particular corpus. No human writes a question and no labelled set is needed — the only input is the document.

The economics

The pattern that works: use the expensive model to generate a few thousand high-quality examples for one specific task, fine-tune a small open model on them, then serve that locally. Your inference cost approaches zero, latency drops, the data stays on your machine, and — with 128GB of unified memory — you can run the result comfortably.

It is also the escalation argument arrived at from a different direction: most work does not need frontier capability, and the trick is having something cheap that is good enough for the common case.

The two things that biteFirst, licensing — many providers' terms restrict using their outputs to train competing models. Check the terms for your teacher before building on this; it's a real constraint, not a formality. Second, the student inherits the teacher's mistakes, confidently and without the teacher's occasional self-correction. Evaluate the student on its own held-out set, never against the teacher's answers.

Where it sits relative to the alternatives

Prompt engineering changes nothing about the model and costs tokens per call. Fine-tuning adapts a model to your format and domain. Distillation transfers capability from a bigger model into a smaller one. Quantization shrinks the same model by storing it less precisely. They compose — a distilled, fine-tuned, 4-bit student is a perfectly normal production artefact, and Block 7 builds toward exactly that.

General-purpose assistants covering many domains
