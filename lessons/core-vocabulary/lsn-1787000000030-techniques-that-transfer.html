Techniques That Transfer, and the Ones That Change Value

Most of what makes a frontier harness good is not the model. It is portable — and several techniques are worth more locally than they are where they were invented.

The previous lesson separates the capability you cannot reproduce from the constraints you might prefer. This one is about the large space in between: the engineering around the model, which is where a great deal of the observed quality difference actually lives and which costs nothing to copy.

The useful move is not to ask whether a technique works locally. Nearly all of them do. It is to ask whether it is worth more or less locally than where you read about it, because the answer is frequently more, and for reasons that follow directly from the hardware lessons in this course.

Worth more locally than hosted

Progressive disclosure
Because your prefill is slower
Deferring tool schemas and skill bodies saves tokens everywhere. On a hosted endpoint those tokens are a line on an invoice; locally they are seconds of wall clock, because prefill on one machine runs at a fraction of a datacentre's rate. The same reduction that trims a hosted bill can be the difference between a usable local loop and an unusable one.
Prefix and KV reuse
Because you can see it and control it
A hosted cache is a feature with a lifetime you do not set. A local engine with true prefix matching lets you keep a session warm on your terms — and the caching lesson's own measurement is a local one, where reuse turned a multi-minute turn into roughly a tenth of that.
Speculative decoding
Because you are batch size one
It buys latency by spending spare compute, so it wins exactly where the machine is otherwise waiting on memory, which is the single-user local case. A busy hosted server has already sold that headroom to batching, which is why this is a much larger lever on your desk than in a datacentre.
Grammars and constrained decoding
Because you own the sampler
The strongest rung of the structured-output ladder is available locally and largely not hosted. This is the one place where the local stack is not catching up but genuinely ahead, and a system that needs guaranteed-parseable output has a real reason to run its own.

Transfers unchanged

Sub-agent isolation
A process boundary, not a product
Running the expensive search somewhere else and returning a conclusion needs no vendor feature at all. Locally it is a second process against the same weights, and the context saving is identical.
The evaluation discipline
Entirely portable
Fixtures, baselines, held-out sets, paired comparison, guardrails. None of it depends on where the model runs, and it is what tells you whether any of the above helped.
Approval gates and least privilege
Security is not a hosted feature
Everything in the security block is harness work. A local agent with a shell tool and no egress control is exactly as dangerous as a hosted one, and rather easier to give too much access to because it is already inside your network.
Structured note-taking and compaction
Same arithmetic, sharper stakes
Context management transfers directly and matters more, because your window is usually smaller and your prefill slower. The techniques are unchanged; the pressure is higher.

Worth less, or differently

Very long contexts
The one to be sceptical about
Filling a large window locally is expensive twice: the key-value cache is what usually exhausts your memory, and the prefill is slow. Retrieval and note-taking are not merely alternatives here, they are frequently the only workable option — which flips the usual hosted advice that a big window can substitute for good retrieval.
Reasoning-heavy prompting
Bounded by what the model was trained to do
Chain-of-thought and inference-time loops help a small local model far less than a frontier one, because the underlying reasoning was never trained in. Spending three times the tokens to get a slightly better wrong answer is a real outcome, and it is worth measuring before adopting a technique on reputation.
Elaborate self-critique
Weakest exactly where it is most tempting
Unaided self-correction degrades correct answers even on strong models. On a small one the critic is the same weak model, so the loop mostly adds cost and variance. Verifier-guided retry — where something outside the model checks — still works and is the version to reach for.
Worth knowing

Most of the gap between a good agent and a bad one is harness, and harness is portable. Copy that before concluding you need a bigger model.

Prompt assembly, tool design, context management, retrieval quality, output validation, retry policy, approval gates — none of these are capabilities of the model and all of them are being done well by the systems people admire. It is the cheapest available improvement and the one most often skipped in favour of a model upgrade, because a model upgrade is a decision and harness work is a fortnight.

For exampleThe agent lesson makes the same point from the other direction with a rule worth repeating here: if a bigger model would fix it, it is a model problem; if better tools, clearer instructions or a shorter context would fix it, it is a harness problem. Establish which one you have before spending money on the other.

The order to work through it

Harness first, because it is free and portable and you will need it either way. Then measure against a frontier model on your own eval set, so the gap is a number rather than an impression. Then decide whether the remaining difference is worth what closing it costs — which may be a hybrid, may be a bigger local model, and may be accepting the gap because the constraint that put you on local hardware has not moved.

The one order that reliably wastes money is the reverse: upgrade the model, find the improvement disappointing, and only then discover the tool descriptions were ambiguous and half the context was distractors.
