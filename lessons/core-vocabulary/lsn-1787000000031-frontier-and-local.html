Frontier and Local, and What Each Is Actually For

Not a ladder with one at the top. Two different sets of constraints, and the choice is which set you would rather have.

This course has taught local serving in detail — what fits, what is fast, what quantisation costs — and it has taught hosted APIs in detail, including the ones that fail. What it has not done is put them side by side, which leaves the impression that local is the budget option and a frontier model is what you use when you can afford it. That framing is wrong often enough to be worth dismantling.

What a frontier model has that you cannot reproduce

Be honest about this half first, because the local case is stronger when it is not overstated. Frontier models are trained with compute and data budgets that are not available to you at any price you would pay, and several capabilities follow from that scale rather than from cleverness you could copy.

Reasoning trained against verifiers
The expensive kind of training
Block 7's reinforcement fine-tuning, run at a scale that requires thousands of accelerators and a very large verified problem set. This is the capability gap that widened most in recent years, and it is the least reproducible.
Long context that works
Not just a large number
A large window is easy to advertise and hard to make usable. The position-bias lesson applies to every model; the difference is how far into the window a model still retrieves reliably, and that is bought with training rather than configuration.
Tool-use reliability
Under compounding
A model that emits a valid tool call 97 per cent of the time and one that manages 99.5 look similar in a demo and behave completely differently over a twenty-step agent loop, where the difference compounds into a large gap in completed tasks.
Breadth you did not plan for
The long tail of requests
A general model handles the question nobody anticipated. A small local model is frequently excellent on the task you tuned it for and poor one step outside it, which matters when real users arrive.

What local has that no amount of money buys from a vendor

The data never leaves
The one that ends arguments
For regulated, confidential or personal material this is not a preference, it is the entire reason the option exists. No contract makes a third party as safe as not sending it, and this alone decides a great many architectures.
Fixed cost, not marginal
The economics invert at volume
Hardware is paid for once and then the tokens are free. A workload with high, steady volume and modest quality requirements can be dramatically cheaper locally; a spiky, low-volume workload is almost always cheaper hosted. The crossover is arithmetic, not ideology.
Nothing is deprecated under you
The version is yours
A hosted model can change beneath a system that was working, and the sampling controls this course documented are being removed rather than added. A local weights file behaves the same next year as it does today, which is worth something to anything that must be reproducible.
You own the sampler
Where local is genuinely ahead
Constrained decoding against a grammar, logit bias, arbitrary sampling parameters, deterministic seeds, log probabilities. Several of these are unavailable from major hosted APIs at any price, and the structured-output lesson's strongest rung is the one only a local stack can offer.
Worth knowing

The question is never which model is better. It is which constraint you are unwilling to accept.

Capability, privacy, cost shape, reproducibility and control are five axes and no option leads on all of them. A workload where confidentiality is non-negotiable has already chosen, whatever the benchmark says. A workload where the hard part is genuinely difficult reasoning has also already chosen, whatever the invoice says. Most real systems have one axis that dominates, and finding it is the actual design decision — the rest is implementation.

For exampleA team compares a local 8B and a frontier model on their eval set, finds a fourteen-point gap, and concludes local is not viable. The material they process cannot legally leave their premises. The comparison was never a decision; the only real question was whether the local option clears their quality bar, and against what fallback for the cases it does not.

The arrangement most systems actually end up with

Framing it as a choice at all is the last thing to give up. The routing lesson makes the argument in general terms and it lands here specifically: run the cheap local path first, detect when it is out of its depth, and escalate. What makes this work is that the detection is usually easier than the task — a schema that fails to validate, a refusal, a confidence signal, a verifier that says no.

The hybrid also inverts the privacy argument in a way worth noticing. If the local model handles the ordinary cases and only the hard ones escalate, then only the hard ones leave, and you have reduced the exposure surface rather than eliminated it. Whether that is acceptable is a question for someone other than the engineer, and it should be asked explicitly rather than discovered in an audit.

Two comparisons that are not fair, and both are common

Comparing a quantised local model against a frontier model at full precision, and calling the gap the local penalty. Some of that gap is the quantisation and some is the model, and the compression lesson says the loss is uneven — so the aggregate difference tells you very little about where you will actually be hurt. Slice it.

Comparing the local model you can run against the frontier model as it is today. Local capability at a given hardware budget has moved quickly, and a conclusion drawn eighteen months ago about what fits on one machine is unlikely to still hold. Re-run the comparison rather than inheriting it, which is cheap because you kept the eval set.
