Reinforcement, Feedback and Humans in the Loop

How a raw model becomes a useful one — and where human judgement is inserted.

A freshly pretrained model predicts plausible text. It isn't helpful, isn't safe, and doesn't follow instructions. Everything that makes it usable comes from a second stage of training driven by feedback — and understanding that stage explains a great deal of model behaviour that otherwise looks arbitrary.

The three learning paradigms

Supervised
Learn from labelled examples
Here's an input, here's the right answer. Most classical ML in Block 2.
Unsupervised
Find structure without labels
Clustering, embeddings. Nobody said what the groups are.
Reinforcement
Learn from consequences
No right answer given — just a signal about whether an outcome was good. The paradigm behind everything in this lesson.

RLHF, in the order it happens

RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback — is the four stages above. Two are worth dwelling on. In stage 3 humans rank model outputs against each other, and a reward model is trained to predict those rankings. In stage 4 the language model is optimised to score highly against that reward model. Humans are no longer in the loop; a learned approximation of them is.

DPO: the same goal, less machinery

Direct Preference Optimization skips the separate reward model and RL stage, training directly on preference pairs — this answer was preferred to that one. Simpler, cheaper, more stable, and now the common default for smaller-scale alignment work. Your LLMS_from_Scratch copy covers it in ch07, so Block 7 uses it in practice.

reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) and Constitutional AI

Human ranking is slow and expensive. RLAIF replaces the human rater with a model. Constitutional AI is a specific form: the model critiques and revises its own outputs against a written set of principles — a constitution — and that self-critique produces the preference data. It's how Claude is trained, and it turns alignment into something partly legible: you can read the principles.

What &ldquo;alignment&rdquo; means

Alignment is how closely a model's behaviour matches what its builders and users intended, including declining what they would not want done. Everything in this lesson is a way of pushing a model toward it. It is a property you measure for a given model and task, not a switch that is on or off, and measuring it is what jailbreaks and red-teaming are for: a jailbreak is a test of how far the alignment holds under pressure. Do not confuse it with the cross-modal alignment from the embeddings lesson, which is about matching images to their captions.

Where feedback goes wrong

  • Reward hacking. The model optimises the measure rather than the goal. If raters prefer longer answers, you get longer answers, not better ones.
  • Sycophancy. Raters prefer agreement, so models learn to agree. This is a trained-in behaviour, not a personality quirk, and it's why a model telling you your idea is excellent is weak evidence.
  • Distribution narrowing. Heavy preference tuning makes outputs safer and more uniform, and less useful at the edges.
  • Feedback on your own outputs. Training on data your own model generated compounds its errors. The same trap as evaluating a model with itself as judge.
The connection to overfittingReward hacking is overfitting wearing different clothes. The model fits the measurement — rater preference — rather than the thing being measured. Same failure, same defence: hold something back that the optimiser can't see.

Human-in-the-loop, and its neighbours

In the loop
Human approves before it happens
The action waits. Slowest, safest. Correct for anything irreversible or outward-facing.
On the loop
Human watches, can intervene
The action proceeds; a person monitors and can stop it. Needs real observability to mean anything.
Out of the loop
Nobody watching
Appropriate only where actions are reversible, bounded and logged.

The word people use loosely is "human-in-the-loop" for what is really human-on-the-loop. The distinction is whether the action waits. If a person can only find out afterwards, they are not in the loop, and the system needs to be safe without them.

The rule that followsMatch the position to reversibility, not to how much you trust the model. Reversible and cheap: out of the loop is fine. Irreversible or outward-facing — sending, publishing, deleting, paying, merging — a human approves first, however good the model has been so far.

Where this reappears

Worth knowing

Match the human's position to reversibility, not to how much you trust the model.

Trust accumulated over a thousand good runs tells you nothing about the thousand-and-first, and the cost of being wrong is set by the action, not by the track record. Reversible and cheap: out of the loop is fine. Irreversible or outward-facing: a human approves first.

For exampleAn agent that drafts replies and saves them for review needs no gate — a bad draft costs someone thirty seconds. The same agent, sending directly, needs one every time, because a wrong email to a customer cannot be recalled and the agent's excellent record last quarter does not change that.
