Context Engineering: What Goes In Decides What Comes Out

For a model you've already chosen, context is the largest lever you still control.

Lesson 2 covered the mechanics of the context window — tokens, limits, the KV cache. This lesson is about the discipline built on top of it, and it may be the highest-leverage skill in the whole program. Once you've picked a model, almost everything that separates a good result from a bad one is what you put in front of it.

The uncomfortable finding: more context is not better

The intuition is that a bigger window means you should fill it. The evidence says otherwise. Liu and colleagues placed the passage containing the answer at different points in a long context and measured how often the model got the question right: accuracy is highest when it sits at the beginning or the end, and falls markedly in between. This is the "lost in the middle" effect, and it has been reproduced across model families. Note what was measured — task accuracy by position, not attention weights, which is a separate finding covered below.

The practical consequencePut the instruction and the single most relevant material at the start or the end — never the middle. If retrieval returns twelve chunks and one is the answer, ranking matters more than recall. Retrieving more and hoping is the most common retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) mistake there is.

Distractors cost more than empty space

Irrelevant-but-plausible context actively degrades answers. A retrieved passage about the 2023 policy, sitting next to the 2026 one, doesn't get politely ignored — it competes. Precision in retrieval beats volume, which is why Block 3 spends time on ranking and filtering rather than on cramming the window.

Where the U-shape comes from, and why it is not a law

It is tempting to file this as a fixed property of transformers and design around it forever. The follow-up work says something more useful and more precise. Two architectural forces pull in opposite directions: causal masking biases attention towards earlier positions, because a token early in the sequence has every later token attending back to it and so gets repeated opportunity to shape what follows; relative positional encodings pull the other way, towards whatever is nearby. The interaction of the two produces the position bias real models show.

The part worth knowing is what completes the explanation. Wu and colleagues find the U-shape emerging when the training data itself carries a bias towards first and last positions — and report that the phenomenon does not appear when the training data lacks that bias. So this is a property of how current models were built and trained, not something the architecture makes inevitable.

Worth knowing

Lost in the middle is a measured property of particular models, not a law of attention. Treat it as something to verify rather than assume.

It has been reproduced widely enough to be a sound default, and it has a known cause and an active fix: Hsieh and colleagues show the same models attend to the ends regardless of relevance, and that calibrating that bias away recovers up to fifteen percentage points on retrieval-augmented tasks. A finding with a mechanism and a remedy is one that newer models can be better at, which means the honest move is to measure position sensitivity on the model you are actually using rather than inheriting a curve from a 2023 paper.

For exampleThe measurement is cheap. Take twenty questions whose answer sits in one known chunk, run each with that chunk placed first, in the middle, and last, and compare accuracy across the three positions. If your model is flat, ordering is not your problem and you can stop ranking for it. If it is U-shaped, you have your own curve rather than someone else's.

None of this changes what you should do

The practical advice survives every one of these refinements intact, because it was never really about the shape of the curve: send less, rank better, and put what matters at an end. That is right if attention is U-shaped, right if it is flat, and right if your model is one of the ones that has been calibrated — a short well-ordered context beats a long one for reasons of cost and distraction that have nothing to do with position bias at all.

What changes is the confidence with which you should state the cause, and whether you would notice if the effect went away.

The context budget

Every turn you are allocating a fixed budget across competing claims:

Instructions
Stable, goes first
System prompt, role, output format. Unchanging, so it also caches well.
Tools
Definitions and schemas
Costs real tokens. Twenty tools you never call is twenty tools' worth of budget spent every turn.
History
Grows without limit if you let it
The main source of runaway cost. Needs active management.
Retrieved material
Should be small and ranked
Precision over volume.
Tool results
Frequently the worst offender
A raw API response or a full file dump can swamp everything else.
Room to answer
Reserved, not leftover
Fill the window and the model has nowhere to write.
The one people missTool results are context. An agent that reads a 3,000-line file has just spent most of its window on it, and every subsequent turn re-sends it. Truncate, summarise or extract before appending. Deterministic retrieval — fetching the relevant slice rather than the whole file — exists precisely for this.

Managing history

  • Sliding window. Keep the last N turns. Simple; loses early decisions.
  • Summarisation / compaction. Periodically replace old turns with a summary. Keeps the thread; loses detail, and errors in the summary propagate.
  • Structured memory. Extract durable facts to a store and retrieve them when relevant, instead of carrying everything forward. More work, and what durable session memory is for.

The three techniques for going long

The management options above are the basics. Once an agent runs for hours rather than turns, three techniques do the heavy lifting, and they are worth knowing by name because they compose:

Compaction
Summarise and reinitiate
As the window fills, replace the trace with a summary preserving decisions made and issues still open, then continue. Tune the compaction prompt for recall first and precision second — losing a decision is worse than carrying redundancy.
Structured note-taking
Persist outside the window
The agent writes durable notes to a file and reads them back after a reset. Memory that survives compaction because it was never in the context to begin with.
Sub-agent isolation
Explore in a clean window
A sub-agent does the expensive exploration in its own context and returns a condensed summary — typically one to two thousand tokens. The main agent gets the finding without the search that produced it.
Clearing tool results is the cheapest winOf all forms of compaction, dropping old tool results is the safest and lightest-touch — they are usually the largest thing in the window, and once acted on they are rarely needed verbatim. Do this before reaching for summarisation, which risks losing something.

The framing that ties these together is an attention budget: not a storage limit but a finite capacity to attend, degrading as a gradient rather than a cliff. Good context engineering is finding the smallest set of high-signal tokens that gets the outcome — a different objective from filling the window.

Context rot

Long agent sessions degrade in a characteristic way: early mistakes stay in the history, get re-read every turn, and are treated as established fact. A wrong assumption at turn 3 is still shaping behaviour at turn 30. Restarting with a clean context and a short accurate summary frequently outperforms continuing — the reason a fresh session sometimes solves what a long one couldn't.

Why this is a named discipline now

"Prompt engineering" described writing a good instruction. Context engineering describes deciding what the model sees at all — retrieval, ranking, compaction, tool-result shaping, ordering, and budget. In an agent, that's most of the harness, which is another way of arriving at the conclusion you'd already reached: the constraint is usually the harness, not the model.

Worth knowing

Put the instruction and the most relevant material at the start or the end — never the middle.

Attention over long context is U-shaped: material at the beginning and end is used reliably, material in the middle much less so. This holds across model families and it means ranking matters more than recall.

For exampleYou retrieve twelve chunks and the answer is in chunk seven. The model says it cannot find the information. Nothing is broken — the chunk is there, in the region the model attends to least. Reranking so the best chunk lands first fixes it without changing the model, the prompt or the retriever.
