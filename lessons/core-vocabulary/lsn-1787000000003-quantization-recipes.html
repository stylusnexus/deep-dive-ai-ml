Quantization Recipes: Beyond Plain Rounding

The same number of bits, chosen better. What the recipes are, what they cost, how to make your own, and where to get them already made.

The quantization lesson ended with a loss: fewer bits means some precision gone in a large language model (LLM). How much is lost depends less on how many bits than on how they were chosen. Plain rounding, called round-to-nearest (RTN), treats every weight alike. A recipe spends some extra effort, usually a little sample text, to lose less at the same size. The best known are AWQ (activation-aware weight quantization), GPTQ (post-training quantization for generative pre-trained transformers) and DWQ (distilled weight quantization). This lesson covers the recipes you will meet, the evidence for and against them, and how to use or make one. The Quantize It Yourself notebook in Block 9 is the hands-on companion.

What goes wrong with plain rounding

A weight is rounded to the nearest of a few levels. The levels are spread across the range of its group, so one large weight stretches the range and every ordinary weight in that group is rounded coarsely. The notebook shows it in one group: a single outlier raised the error on the other 63 weights from 9% to 49%. The AWQ paper adds the second half of the story: Protecting only 1% salient weights can greatly reduce quantization error, and the salient ones are found by looking at the activations, not the weights. A weight that is small but multiplied by a large activation matters more than a large weight that is multiplied by almost nothing.

  • On a very large model. The GPTQ paper reports OPT-175B (Meta's Open Pre-trained Transformer, 175 billion parameters) on WikiText-2 (a standard text benchmark), perplexity 8.34 at full precision. At 4 bits, GPTQ 8.37 and RTN 10.54. At 3 bits, GPTQ 8.68 and RTN 7,300, which the paper describes as RTN collapsing completely. Measured by the authors of GPTQ.
  • On a 7-billion-parameter model. The AWQ paper, group size 128, Llama-2-7B: full precision 5.47. At 3 bits RTN 6.66, GPTQ 6.43, AWQ 6.24. At 4 bits RTN 5.73, GPTQ 5.69, AWQ 5.60. Measured by the authors of AWQ.
  • The honest part. The same paper says RTN is actually quite strong when using a small group size like 128. At 4 bits on a mid-sized model the gap is small. The recipes earn their keep at 3 bits and below, and where you cannot afford small groups.

The recipes, one line each

RTN
Round to nearest, group by group
The baseline. It needs no data and takes seconds. It is what mlx_lm.convert -q does, and what a plain -4bit model on the Hub usually is.
GPTQ
Weights that keep each layer&#39;s output close
In the words of the MLX (Apple's machine-learning framework) documentation, it “finds quantized weights which minimize the squared error of each layer's output given the provided input”. Needs calibration text. The MLX docs put its run time at “from a few minutes to several hours”.
AWQ
Scale first, then round
“Scales and clips the weights prior to quantization”, using the activations to decide which channels to protect. Needs far less calibration text than GPTQ: on one model the AWQ paper reports good results with 16 sequences against 192.
DWQ
Train the scales to match the teacher
“Fine-tunes non-quantized parameters (including quantization scales and biases) using the non-quantized model as a teacher.” The MLX docs say it works best for 2-bit to 4-bit and “often doesn't work well” when distilling to 8-bit or 6-bit. Slower than dynamic quantization, and typically better.
Dynamic quantization
More bits where it hurts
Estimate how sensitive each layer is and give the sensitive ones more bits. mlx_lm.dynamic_quant defaults to 5 bits for sensitive layers and 4 for the rest. mlx_lm.convert --quant-predicate mixed_4_6 is a fixed version: more bits for the attention value and feed-forward output projections in the first and last eighth of the layers and every third in between, plus the output head. llama.cpp's Q4_K_M is the same idea, in its own scheme.
Importance matrix (imatrix)
llama.cpp&#39;s sample-text recipe
Run sample text through the full-precision model, record how large the activations are on each input channel, and use that as weights when choosing how to round. It is produced with llama-imatrix and used with llama-quantize --imatrix.
Unsloth Dynamic (UD)
A vendor&#39;s per-model schemes
In the Dynamic 2.0 section of its documentation Unsloth says it now dynamically adjust[s] the quantization type of every possible layer with a scheme tailored to each model, and calibrates on a curated set of more than 1.5 million tokens. It sells and promotes its own tools and its comparisons are self-measured. A UD-Q4_K_M file is not published for every model.

Why bother: what the measurements show

Three kinds of evidence, in rising order of how much to trust them on your model: the authors' papers (above), published measurements by others, and your own. The second:

  • llama.cpp's importance matrix. From the llama.cpp perplexity README, Llama-3 8B on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 graphics card, scored on WikiText-2, with the matrix made from WikiText too, which probably flatters it. Full precision scores 6.233. A q3_K_M file: perplexity 6.888 and KL divergence (Kullback–Leibler divergence, how far one probability distribution has moved from another) 0.1019 without a matrix, 6.734 and 0.0844 with one. A q4_K_M file: 6.407 and 0.0313, then 6.383 and 0.0282. The gain is larger at fewer bits.
  • On MLX, one blogger's measurements. On the Qwen3.6 35B-A3B mixture-of-experts model, KL divergence against an 8-bit reference on an M5 Max: plain 4-bit 0.074, DWQ 4-bit 0.027. On the 27B model, plain 4-bit 0.113 against 0.059 for Unsloth's MLX build, which that post also measured at 8.6 effective bits per weight and about twice as far from the original as a plain 6-bit file. The author wrote the measuring tool and the post has no peer review. It is a single run, and a reminder that a clever name is not a guarantee.

And our own, using scripts/measure_quantization.py on public Qwen3-0.6B files against the 16-bit original, on the course's own documents, 8 windows of 512 tokens each:

model                          MB   bits/w  perplexity       KL  top-1 same
16-bit original              1192    16.00      35.151   0.0000     100.0%
4bit  (plain)                 335     4.50      41.910   0.2337      73.7%
4bit-AWQ                      345     4.63      38.752   0.1420      78.6%
4bit-DWQ                      335     4.50      40.299   0.3277      76.8%

The AWQ file is best on all three measures, and also uses 4.63 bits per weight against 4.50, so some of its win may be bits. The DWQ file beats plain rounding on perplexity and on how often it picks the same word, and is worse on KL divergence. The three measures disagree about DWQ. We did not find out why. It may be that its calibration text was chat while ours is incident reports, or that the model is tiny, or something else. That disagreement is the finding: pick your measure, and your text, before you look.

Worth knowing

Compare quantizations at equal bits per weight and judge them on your own text, because the label hides both.

“4-bit” names a bit width, not a cost. MLX files in this course are 4.50 bits per weight, the AWQ file above is 4.63, and the Q4_K_M file we made from a 0.5-billion-parameter model is 6.35. Perplexity on a standard text can stay flat while the model starts choosing different words, and a recipe tuned on one kind of text can lose on another.

For exampleThe notebook's own results show the shape on a small model, Qwen2.5-0.5B. Dropping from 6.5 to 4.5 bits per weight multiplied the KL divergence by about 12 (0.018 to 0.219), and 3.5 bits multiplied it by about six more (1.24), with the model picking the same next word only 43% of the time. At 2.5 bits it was no longer a working model: perplexity 217,328.

Accuracy can hold still while the answers change

The paper “Accuracy is Not All You Need” (Microsoft Research) reports that compressed models can match the original on accuracy while many individual answers flip between right and wrong, in what it calls flips. It reports accuracy differences of 0–2% against flips of up to 13.6%, and proposes KL divergence and the share of flips as the better measures. A model that scores the same on average has not necessarily kept your task intact. Test your own task.

Do you have to start from scratch?

Almost never. The recipes above are implemented in maintained tools, and most popular models already have quantizations made by someone. What exists:

  • MLX (Apple Silicon): mlx_lm.dwq --model Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B, mlx_lm.awq, mlx_lm.gptq and mlx_lm.dynamic_quant. Install with pip install "mlx-lm[train]". All use calibration data to tune parameters. By default mlx_lm.awq, gptq and dynamic_quant download one public calibration text and sample random chunks of it, while mlx_lm.dwq defaults to a Hugging Face chat dataset, allenai/tulu-3-sft-mixture, which is part of why a DWQ file may be tuned for conversation rather than your text.
  • llama.cpp (GGUF, the GPT-Generated Unified Format it reads): llama-imatrix -m model-f16.gguf -f calibration.txt -ngl 99, then llama-quantize --imatrix imatrix.gguf model-f16.gguf out.gguf q4_k_m.
  • NVIDIA and vLLM: LLM Compressor (from the vLLM project, which saves in its compressed-tensors format), GPTQModel (from the company ModelCloud) and NVIDIA's Model Optimizer (vendor-run and NVIDIA-focused). AutoAWQ is archived, and its README says it is deprecated and points users to LLM Compressor and mlx-lm.

What we ran ourselves: mlx_lm.convert and llama-quantize, in the notebook. The commands above for DWQ, AWQ, GPTQ and imatrix are from the tools' own documentation and were not run here. The first DWQ attempt failed because it needs the extra datasets package that the [train] install adds, and this llama.cpp build does not include llama-imatrix.

When you do need your own, what to know

  • Start from the 16-bit original, once. llama-quantize refuses to requantize an already-quantized file unless you pass --allow-requantize, and warns that it “can severely reduce quality compared to quantizing from 16bit or 32bit”. The notebook shows the refusal. We found no published measurement of how much it costs, so that is vendor guidance, not a number.
  • Calibration text should look like your use, and must not be your test. The AWQ paper found matching the calibration and evaluation domain worked best. On a different domain AWQ lost 0.5–0.6 perplexity while GPTQ lost 2.3–4.9, on one model and two domains. GPTQ's paper used 128 random 2048-token segments of a web text collection. We found no study that measures domain-matched calibration on downstream task accuracy.
  • Give sensitive layers more bits. The output head and embeddings are the usual ones. MLX's preset gives the head extra bits, llama.cpp has --leave-output-tensor and --token-embedding-type, and the blog post above suggests that the router of a mixture-of-experts model is sensitive enough that saving bits there costs more than it saves. It did not isolate the router, so treat that as a lead.
  • Measure distance, not just perplexity. llama.cpp says the loss is usually measured in perplexity and KL divergence, and warns that perplexity is not comparable across models with different tokenizers. Its KL workflow saves the full-precision model's outputs to a file that it says is 37 GiB for Llama 3 on WikiText-2, so budget disk.
  • Write the recipe down. The tool and its version, the base model revision, the calibration text, the settings, and the type of each tensor. You will want it when the numbers surprise you.

If your architecture is new, check two things before anything else. Does the tool support its layers? And does the engine you will serve with have a fast kernel for the format you are about to produce? A format with no fast kernel runs slowly or not at all, as the formats lesson explains.

Where to get recipes and ready-made quantizations

  • mlx-community on Hugging Face publishes MLX files, and its cards are terse. The Qwen3-0.6B -4bit, -4bit-AWQ and -4bit-DWQ cards we read are word for word the same: converted to MLX format from the base model using mlx-lm 0.24.0. The recipe appears only in the file name. A card with no recipe named should be assumed to be plain rounding.
  • unsloth publishes GGUF files, including the UD- ones, with its recipe documented. Self-measured, and not every model has every variant: the Qwen3-Coder 30B repository has UD-*_XL files and no UD-Q4_K_M.
  • bartowski, an individual who publishes GGUF files. The Llama 3.1 card we read names the llama.cpp release used, says every file was made with an importance matrix and links its dataset, and describes its Q6_K_L variant as keeping embedding and output weights at Q8_0.
  • lmstudio-community publishes GGUF and MLX files. Cards vary: some name the llama.cpp release and say nothing about an importance matrix, and the Llama 3.1 card credits a dataset used to calculate one. LM Studio says it does not guarantee community models.
  • The model makers themselves. Qwen publishes GGUF, GPTQ and AWQ versions of its models. Official cards are not free of mistakes: the Qwen2.5-7B AWQ card says 72B in its text.

How to judge a file before you trust it (our inference from the cards above, not an official standard): does the card name the base model and revision, the tool and its version, the recipe, and the calibration data? Does it say which type each file is? A card that names none of these is a file you should score yourself. You can, with one command:

python3 scripts/measure_quantization.py --reference mlx-community/Qwen3-0.6B-bf16 \
    --model mlx-community/Qwen3-0.6B-4bit --model mlx-community/Qwen3-0.6B-4bit-AWQ
