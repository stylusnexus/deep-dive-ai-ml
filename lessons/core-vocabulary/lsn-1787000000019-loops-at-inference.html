Loops at Inference: Control Flow, Limits and Dangers

Running the model more than once — when that buys accuracy, and when it just buys tokens.

The single most common way to improve an LLM system is to stop calling the model once. Sample several times, critique and revise, retry on failure, search over branches. All of these are loops, and they are the main lever you have that doesn't involve changing the model.

They also fail in a way worth naming up front, because it predicts which ones work.

The rule that governs all of itA loop improves the result only if signal enters from outside the loop. A compiler, a test suite, a schema validator, a retrieval hit, or the independence of separate samples — something that isn't the model's own opinion. A loop with no external signal mostly amplifies whatever the model already believed, including its errors. Every result below is explained by that one sentence.

The control flow

Strip the branding and there are four shapes. What differs is who decides to go round again.

What actually works

Verifier-guided retry
Strongest
Generate, run the tests, feed failures back. The verifier is ground truth the model can't argue with. This is why coding agents work better than most agentic domains — the loop has a compiler in it.
Self-consistency
Reliable, expensive
Sample several independent chains at non-zero temperature and take the majority answer. Works because the errors are independent and the right answer is reachable by several routes; wrong answers scatter, correct ones agree.
Best-of-N with a reward model
Strong if the scorer is
Generate N, pick the highest-scoring. Quality is capped by the scorer — a weak reward model just picks confidently wrong answers.
Escalation
Cheap-first routing
Try the small model, verify, escalate on failure. It belongs in this family: a loop with an external verifier deciding whether to go round again.

What works less than its reputation suggests

Self-correction without external feedback. Asking a model to review and fix its own answer is intuitively appealing and, on reasoning tasks, frequently makes things worse. Huang et al. found that models often change correct answers to incorrect ones when told to reconsider, because the instruction to find fault is itself a pressure to change something.

Reflexion-style loops do work — but read what they contain: an external signal, usually a test result or environment feedback, converted into words the model can act on. The gain comes from the signal, not from the reflecting.

The test to applyBefore adding a critique loop, ask what enters it that the model didn't already have. If the answer is "nothing, it just looks again", expect little and measure carefully. If the answer is "a failing test", "a schema error", "a retrieval result" — that's a real loop.

The dangers

  • Runaway cost. No ceiling means an agent can retry a failing call until someone notices the bill. Every loop needs a turn limit and a spend limit, not one or the other.
  • Error compounding. Steps at 95% reliability give 60% over ten steps. Long loops fail not because a step is bad but because reliability multiplies.
  • Context poisoning. Each iteration appends to the context. A wrong conclusion at turn 3 is re-read as established fact at turn 30, and the loop entrenches it rather than escaping it.
  • Quadratic cost growth. The whole conversation is re-sent each turn, so a twenty-turn loop is far more than twenty times a single call.
  • False confidence from agreement. Self-consistency across samples that share a systematic bias produces unanimous agreement on the wrong answer. Unanimity is not evidence when the samples aren't independent.
The termination condition is the designMost loop bugs are termination bugs. "Until the model says it's finished" hands the stopping decision to the component least able to judge whether it succeeded — the same problem as letting a model report its own eval score. Terminate on an external check, a turn cap, or a spend cap. Preferably all three.

The name this family goes by now

Everything in this lesson is test-time compute — spending more computation at inference rather than more parameters at training. It is now one of the two main axes of capability improvement alongside scale, and it is the axis you control without training anything.

It works for the reason the whole lesson works: extra passes reach answers a single pass cannot, provided something filters or aggregates across them. The scaling is real and not free — cost grows with the compute you spend, so the engineering question is where on that curve your task sits, not whether more is better.

Where the frontier movedRecent work applies these loops to retrieval itself — retrieve, reason about what came back, decide what is still missing, retrieve again. That fixes multi-hop questions a single retrieval cannot answer, and introduces its own failure: repeated near-identical retrievals burning turns and tokens without adding information. De-duplicating what has already been retrieved, and contextualising results before reasoning over them, are the current mitigations. Same lesson, new surface — a loop needs external signal and a termination condition, and retrieval loops are no exception.

Choosing a loop

Match the loop to whether the task has a checkable outcome. Code, structured output and retrieval have verifiers, so verifier-guided retry is available and should be the default. Open-ended prose has none, so you're left with self-consistency or best-of-N, and you should expect smaller gains for more tokens. If a task has no external signal at all, extra iterations mostly buy latency.

Further reading

  • Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2022)Sampling several chains and taking the majority. The clearest example of a loop whose signal is sample independence.
  • LLMs Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet (Huang et al., 2023)The evidence that unaided self-critique often degrades correct answers. Read before building a reflection loop.
  • Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023)Self-reflection that works — note that the loop carries environment feedback, not just introspection.
  • Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023)Search over branches rather than a single chain, and the cost of doing it.
  • Agentic RAG: a survey (Singh et al., 2025)Taxonomy of retrieval loops by agent count, control structure and autonomy — the map of this design space.
  • Test-Time Strategies for Agentic RAG (2026)The failure modes of retrieval loops — repetitive retrieval, weak contextualisation — and the de-duplication fix.
