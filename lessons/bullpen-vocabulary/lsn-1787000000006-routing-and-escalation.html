Routing, Escalation and Sampling Controls

The idea Bullpen is built on — plus temperature and top-k, which decide how a model picks words.

Escalation appears 44 times in Bullpen's docs. It is not a feature of the project; it is the project's whole argument.

Predict versus try

Commercial model routers try to predict, from your prompt, which model will handle it best. Bullpen's premise is that this is unreliable — models produce confident wrong answers, and so do the classifiers predicting them.

The inversion: try the cheap local model, watch what happens, and escalate only when the result actually fails. Prediction is replaced by observation. You pay a little latency on hard tasks in exchange for not paying frontier prices on easy ones.

Why this only works with a verifierEscalation-on-failure requires knowing that something failed. Where failure is checkable — code doesn't compile, tests fail, output doesn't match a schema, tool call malformed — the strategy is sound. Where quality is subjective, there's no signal to escalate on, and you're back to guessing. Check what your failure detector actually detects before trusting a routing scheme.

What escalation costs

  • Latency. A failed local attempt is time spent before the real answer starts.
  • Tokens. You pay for the failed attempt too, though local attempts are near-free.
  • Complexity. Two lanes, two sets of failure modes, and a decision layer that can itself be wrong.

The trade is worth it when most tasks are easy — which, for coding assistance, they are. Routing is an economic bet on the shape of your workload, so it's worth measuring that shape rather than assuming it.

Temperature and top-k: how the next word gets picked

At each step the model produces a probability across every token in its vocabulary. Sampling controls decide how that distribution gets turned into one choice.

Temperature
Flattens or sharpens the distribution
0 means always take the most likely token — deterministic and repetitive. Around 0.7 is a conversational default. Above ~1.2, coherence degrades.
top-k
Consider only the k most likely tokens
Discards the long tail of nonsense before sampling.
top-p
Consider the smallest set summing to probability p
Adapts to the distribution's shape — more choices when the model is unsure, fewer when it's confident. Usually preferred over top-k.
The practical settingFor anything parsed by code — tool calls, structured output, classification, routing — use temperature 0 or very close. You want repeatability, not creativity. Save higher temperatures for prose where variation is the point. A surprising share of 'the agent is flaky' bugs are just an unnecessarily high temperature on a structured task.

Determinism, honestly

Temperature 0 makes sampling deterministic but does not guarantee identical outputs across runs. Batching, hardware differences, and floating-point non-associativity introduce variation, especially with hosted providers. Design evaluations to tolerate small differences rather than assuming byte-identical replies.

✅ Check Yourself

1. Why does Bullpen try a local model first rather than predicting which model to use?

Local models are usually better than frontier models
Prediction is unreliable; observing an actual failure is a stronger signal than guessing in advance
It avoids sending data to third parties

2. When is escalation-on-failure a poor strategy?

When the local model is small
When failure isn't detectable — subjective quality gives you no signal to escalate on
When latency matters at all

3. Your agent's tool calls are intermittently malformed. What should you check first?

Whether temperature is above 0 on a task that needs deterministic structured output
Whether the context window is full
Whether the model supports tool calling
