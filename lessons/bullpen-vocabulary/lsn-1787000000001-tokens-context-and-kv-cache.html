Tokens, the Context Window, and the KV Cache

Why a model 'forgets', why long conversations get expensive, and what's actually being cached.

A model does not read characters or words. It reads tokens — chunks of text produced by a tokenizer, roughly ¾ of a word in English. "unbelievable" might be three tokens; a rare surname might be six. Every cost, limit and speed figure in this field is denominated in tokens, which is why the unit matters.

The context window is a hard wall

The context window is the maximum number of tokens the model can consider at once — system prompt, conversation history, retrieved documents, tool definitions, tool results, and the answer being generated, all counted together.

It is not memory in any human sense. Nothing is remembered between calls. Each time the loop runs, the entire conversation is re-sent. What looks like a model remembering what you said ten turns ago is the harness re-sending those ten turns every single time.

The mistake this causesPeople assume a longer context window means the model 'pays attention' to everything in it. It doesn't. Retrieval quality degrades toward the middle of a long context — the well-documented 'lost in the middle' effect. A 200k window is a capacity limit, not a promise of comprehension.

Why long sessions get expensive fast

Because the whole conversation is re-sent each turn, cost grows quadratically with conversation length, not linearly. Turn 20 sends roughly twenty times the tokens of turn 1. This is the single biggest driver of surprise LLM bills, and it's why Bullpen persists and prunes sessions rather than letting them grow without limit.

The KV cache

When a model processes tokens, each layer computes key and value vectors for every token. Generating the next token requires those vectors for all previous tokens. Recomputing them every step would be enormously wasteful, so they're kept in the KV cache.

Two consequences you can feel:

  • Memory. The KV cache grows with context length and is often what actually exhausts RAM when running a model locally — not the weights. This is why a model that loads fine can still die on a long prompt.
  • Speed. The first token is slow (the whole prompt must be processed — 'prefill'); subsequent tokens are fast (only one new token to process — 'decode'). That's why time-to-first-token and tokens-per-second are reported separately.
Prompt caching is this, soldWhen a provider offers 'prompt caching' at a discount, they are keeping the KV cache for your unchanging prefix instead of recomputing it. That's why the advice is always to put the stable content — system prompt, long documents — at the front and the variable content at the end. Reorder and the cache misses.

2. You load a 13B model locally with room to spare, then it runs out of memory on a long document. What most likely consumed the memory?

The KV cache, which grows with context length
The model weights, which expand during inference
The tokenizer building a vocabulary

3. Why does prompt-cache advice say to put stable content first?

Models pay more attention to the beginning of a prompt
The cache is keyed on an unchanged prefix — change something early and everything after it must be recomputed
It reduces the total token count
