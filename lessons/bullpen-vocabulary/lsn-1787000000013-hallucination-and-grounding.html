Hallucination and Grounding

Why a model states falsehoods with total confidence — and what actually reduces it.

A model asserts that a regulation exists, cites a section number, and summarises its contents. None of it is real. The word for this is hallucination, and understanding why it happens tells you which fixes work and which are theatre.

It isn't lying, and it isn't a bug

A language model samples the next token from a probability distribution. It has no separate store of facts to check against and no internal flag distinguishing "I know this" from "this is the shape a plausible answer would take". A fabricated citation is generated by exactly the same process as a correct one — the model is producing text that fits the pattern of a citation.

The consequence that mattersFluency and accuracy are independent. Confident phrasing carries no information about correctness, because confident phrasing is what the training data looks like. This is why 'the model seemed sure' is never evidence, and why asking a model whether it's certain rarely helps — you get a plausible answer about confidence, generated the same way.

Where hallucination concentrates

  • Specifics the model half-knows. Names, dates, section numbers, statistics, API method signatures. The pattern is familiar; the exact value is not.
  • The long tail. Anything rare in training data. Well-covered topics are far safer than obscure ones.
  • After the cutoff. Events past the training date get answered from pattern rather than knowledge.
  • When the premise is false. Ask about a regulation that doesn't exist and models will frequently describe it rather than challenge you.
  • Under pressure to be complete. Asked for ten examples when six exist, a model will often supply ten.

Grounding

Grounding means tying the model's output to supplied source material rather than to its parameters. Put the actual document in context and ask the model to answer from it. That's what RAG is for, and it's the single most effective mitigation available.

What works, roughly in order

Put the source in context
The biggest single reduction. Most of Block 3.

Makes the claim checkable by a human, and discourages answers with no support.

"Say you don't know"
Costs little. Models default to answering because that's what the training rewarded — you have to actively license the alternative.

Enums, schemas, closed sets

Verify externally

Cheap deterministic checks catch a surprising share of fabrication.
The mitigation that doesn't workTelling the model "do not hallucinate" or "only state facts" achieves close to nothing on its own. The model has no mechanism for distinguishing the two categories at generation time, so the instruction can't be acted on. It shifts tone, not accuracy. Architecture beats exhortation — the same conclusion as prompt injection.

Grounding is not a guarantee

A grounded system can still fail: retrieval returns nothing and the model answers from memory anyway; it returns the wrong passage and the model faithfully summarises the wrong thing; or the model blends retrieved content with parametric knowledge and produces something that half-matches its source. This is why Block 5 measures groundedness — whether the answer is actually supported by what was retrieved — separately from whether the answer is correct.
