Lesson 4 of 5

Agentic RAG: Retrieval as a Loop

When one retrieval cannot answer the question — and the failure modes that arrive with the loop.

Single-shot retrieval answers a question in one hop: embed, retrieve, generate. It works well and it has a hard limit. Some questions cannot be answered by any single retrieval, however good, because the answer does not exist in one place.

The questions single-shot cannot reach

Multi-hop
The answer needs two facts that live apart
"Which site has the turbine with the highest failure rate?" needs per-turbine rates, then a site lookup. Neither chunk contains both.
Comparative
Requires assembling several sources
"How does this year's policy differ from last year's?" needs both policies retrieved and contrasted.
Under-specified
The real question emerges from a first look
"Why did output drop?" cannot be embedded usefully until you know which site and which period.
Aggregative
Needs many chunks, not the top five
"How many incidents mentioned gearbox temperature?" is a counting problem wearing a retrieval costume.
The last one is a warning, not a use caseAggregative questions are usually the wrong shape for retrieval entirely. Counting, filtering and grouping are what a database does exactly and cheaply, and what a retrieval loop does approximately and expensively. If the question is really SQL, write SQL — the deterministic-first argument applies here as much as anywhere.

The other answer to the same question

The loop is one way to reach a fact that lives in two places. The other is to stop searching for the relationship each time and store it: extract the entities and the connections between them from the corpus ahead of time, and the multi-hop question becomes a traversal instead of a search. This is what people mean by a knowledge graph, and by GraphRAG when it is wired into retrieval.

Take the question above. Under retrieval it is two searches and a model holding the intermediate result in its head. Under a graph, turbine and site are nodes, installed at is an edge, the failure rate is a property, and the answer is a query that walks from one to the other — no iteration, no intermediate context, and the same answer every time.

Where it genuinely wins
Questions about relationships, not passages
Anything phrased as which, how many, who else, what connects — where the answer is a join across entities rather than a passage that contains it. Also anything you need to be repeatable, because a traversal is deterministic and a loop is not.
What it costs
An extraction pipeline, and its upkeep
Something has to read the corpus and decide what the entities and relationships are. That is usually a model, run over everything, once per corpus and again whenever the corpus changes.
The failure that matters
Extraction errors become structure
A retrieval mistake affects one answer. A wrong edge in the graph is reused by every question that walks through it, and it is invisible at query time because the traversal is working perfectly on bad data.
How it is usually deployed
Alongside vectors, not instead of them
The graph answers the structural part and the vector index still finds the prose. Most working systems are hybrids, in the same way that Block 3's ranking lesson ends up combining keyword and vector search rather than choosing.
Worth knowing

A knowledge graph moves the work from query time to build time. That is the entire trade.

The loop is cheap to build and expensive per question — every ask pays the turns, the tokens and the latency again, and gets a slightly different path each time. The graph is expensive to build and cheap per question, and gives the same answer twice. Neither is more advanced than the other; they bill differently, and which one is right depends on whether your questions are numerous and repetitive or few and varied.

For exampleA corpus of ten thousand maintenance reports, queried a hundred times a day with questions about which sites and which components. Extracting a graph once costs a few hours of model time and makes every one of those hundred queries a fast, repeatable traversal. The same corpus queried twice a week with open-ended questions does not repay the pipeline, and the loop is the right answer.

The same discipline this lesson already applies to the loop

Everything the lesson says about not making the loop the default applies here with more force, because a graph costs more up front. The majority of questions are single-hop and a well-built vector index answers them. Build the graph when you can point at a class of question you are actually asked, repeatedly, that needs relationships — not because the architecture is appealing.

And be suspicious of a graph built entirely by a model with nobody checking the edges. Extraction quality is the whole system: a graph with plausible wrong relationships is a machine for producing confident wrong answers, and it will be more convincing than a bad retrieval because it arrives with structure attached.

What the loop adds

Retrieve, reason about what came back, decide what is still missing, retrieve again. The model controls the query rather than receiving results of one it never saw.

The failure modes the loop brings with it

  • Repetitive retrieval. The commonest and most expensive. The model reformulates slightly, gets near-identical results, and goes round again — burning turns and tokens without adding information.
  • Weak contextualisation. Results arrive but are not integrated into the reasoning, so the next query is no better informed than the first.
  • Context saturation. Every hop appends more retrieved text. By hop four the window holds a great deal of material, most of it already used, and attention thins across all of it.
  • Drift. Each reformulation moves slightly away from the original question. By the fourth, the system is answering something adjacent.
  • Cost you did not budget. Four hops is four retrievals and four generations, on a growing context. Several times the cost of single-shot, for a question that may not have needed it.
Worth knowing

A retrieval loop with no de-duplication will re-retrieve what it already has.

Slight query reformulations return substantially overlapping results. Without tracking what has already been seen, the loop mistakes repetition for progress, and its context fills with the same passages under different rankings.

For exampleAn agent asked a multi-hop question runs six retrievals. Passages two and three appear in five of the six result sets. The final context holds forty chunks of which fourteen are distinct, and the answer is no better than hop two would have produced at a sixth of the cost.

What makes it work

De-duplicate against what you have
Non-negotiable
Track retrieved chunk ids and exclude them, or explicitly down-weight them, on subsequent hops.
Contextualise before reasoning
Summarise what came back
Turn raw chunks into a short statement of what is now known and what is still missing, then query on that.
Hard hop limit
Three or four
Plus a token budget. The gains flatten fast and the costs do not.
Decompose explicitly
Where the structure is known
"Find the rate, then look up the site" as two planned retrievals beats hoping a loop discovers the same decomposition.
Route by question shape
Not everything needs the loop
Most questions are single-hop. Detect the ones that are not, rather than paying loop cost on everything.
Worth knowing

Route to the loop; do not make it the default.

The overwhelming majority of real questions are answerable in one hop. Running every question through a multi-hop loop multiplies cost and latency across the whole workload to serve the minority that needed it, and adds failure modes to the majority that did not.

For exampleA support assistant handles 2,000 questions a day. Roughly 60 genuinely need multiple hops. Making the loop the default multiplies the daily bill several times over and introduces drift and repetition into 1,940 questions that a single retrieval answered correctly.

The same rule as every other loop

This is the loops lesson arriving at a new surface. A retrieval loop improves things only when signal enters from outside it — a de-duplication check, a coverage test, an explicit "do I now have what I need" evaluated against something other than the model's confidence. Without that, extra hops mostly amplify the framing of the first query.

"Do I have enough information?" is the model marking its own workAsking the model whether it can answer yet is the same circularity as asking an agent whether it succeeded. It will frequently say yes when it has the shape of an answer rather than the substance. Prefer a checkable condition — required entities present, a citation for each part of the question, a hop budget — over the model's self-assessment.
