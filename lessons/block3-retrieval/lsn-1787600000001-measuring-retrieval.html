Measuring Retrieval

Three numbers, measured separately, because they fail independently and have different fixes.

"The RAG system is not very good" is not a measurement. It could be retrieval missing the right chunk, ranking burying it, the index quietly losing candidates, or the model failing to use what it was given. Each has a different fix, and one aggregate score cannot distinguish them.

Measure three things, separately

Index recall
Is the index returning what exact search would?
A property of your approximate index, measured against brute force. Nobody checks this.
Retrieval quality
Is the right chunk in the top k?
A property of embeddings, chunking and ranking. Measured against a labelled set.
Groundedness
Is the answer supported by what was retrieved?
A property of generation. Distinct from whether the answer is correct.

A system can have perfect index recall, poor retrieval quality and high groundedness — faithfully summarising the wrong document. Only measuring the three apart tells you that.

1 · Index recall, in about an hour

Take 200 real queries. Run each through exact brute-force search over the whole corpus and keep the true top-k. Run each through your index. Recall@k is the mean overlap.

This is the number nobody computes, and it silently caps everything above it. An index at 90% recall is discarding one correct result in ten before ranking even begins, and the results it does return look entirely reasonable.

Worth knowing

An approximate index that loses candidates looks exactly like a system that never had them.

Approximate nearest-neighbour search trades accuracy for speed by design. The results it returns are plausible, so nothing about the output signals that better ones were skipped — and every downstream metric inherits the loss without attributing it.

For exampleRAG quality drops after a corpus grows from 50,000 to 400,000 chunks. Two weeks go into prompts, chunk sizes and a new embedding model. The index parameters were tuned at the original size; measured against exact search, recall had fallen from 0.97 to 0.71. One configuration change fixed it.

2 · Retrieval quality

This needs labels: questions paired with the chunks that genuinely answer them. Twenty to fifty is enough to be useful — the same twenty-item argument as everywhere else in this course.

Recall@k
Is the right chunk in the top k?
The headline. Measure at the k you actually send to the model, not at 100.
MRR
Mean reciprocal rank
How high it ranked, averaged. Rewards getting the answer to position one rather than position five.
Hit rate at k=1
Did the best chunk come first?
Blunt and honest. If this is low, a reranker is the highest-value addition.
Build the label set from real questions, not invented onesQuestions you write while looking at the corpus are subtly shaped by what you just read, and they retrieve better than real ones do. Take them from support tickets, search logs or actual users. Twenty real questions beat two hundred invented ones.

3 · Groundedness, and why it is not correctness

Two separate questions, and conflating them hides the more actionable one:

Groundedness
Is every claim supported by the retrieved text?
A failure here is a generation problem. Fixable with prompting, citations, or a smaller context.
Correctness
Is the answer actually right?
A failure here with high groundedness means retrieval gave it the wrong material. Fixable upstream.

The combination is what tells you where to work. High groundedness with low correctness points squarely at retrieval. Low groundedness with high correctness means the model is answering from memory and happening to be right — which is worse than it sounds, because it will not always be.

Worth knowing

High groundedness and low correctness is a retrieval problem. Low groundedness and high correctness is a warning.

The first means the model faithfully used what it was given, and it was given the wrong thing. The second means the model is answering from its parameters and getting away with it, which is not a property you can rely on.

For exampleAn assistant answers a policy question correctly, but the cited passage does not support the claim. Retrieval had failed and the model knew the answer anyway. The evaluation passes on correctness, the system is not actually grounded, and the first question outside the model's knowledge produces a confident fabrication with a citation attached.

The fourth number, which this lesson used to omit

Everything above measures whether retrieval found the right thing. None of it measures how long the request took, and the two are not distributed the way people assume. Break a retrieval request into stages and the shape is stark: embedding the query is a few milliseconds, approximate nearest-neighbour search over a few million vectors is tens of milliseconds, and reranking a shortlist with a cross-encoder is around a hundred. Then the model reads what came back, and on a long context that step alone runs into seconds.

Retrieval is a small fraction of the clock. Tuning it for speed is optimising the part that was already cheap, while the expensive stage sits immediately downstream and is fed by exactly how much you chose to retrieve.

Retrieval time
Milliseconds
Embedding, search and reranking together. Worth measuring, and rarely worth optimising first.
Read time (prefill)
Often seconds
The model processing everything retrieval handed it, before a single output token appears. This is what the user experiences as the delay.
The lever
Send fewer chunks, not find them faster
Read time grows with how much you send. Attention's own cost grows with the square of the context, but that term does not dominate until the context approaches the model's hidden dimension — at retrieval-sized contexts the read is close to linear in tokens. Either way, precision beats volume on the clock for the same reason it beats volume on the answer.
Worth knowing

“Prefill is quadratic” is true about attention and misleading about the read.

Attention does compare every token against every other, so its cost carries a squared term. But attention is only part of prefill: the projections and the feed-forward layers cost roughly tokens times the hidden dimension squared, which is linear in context. The squared term does not overtake the linear one until the context is on the order of the model's hidden dimension — thousands of tokens — so at retrieval-sized contexts the read grows roughly in proportion to what you send.

For exampleProject 2 measures this on its own corpus by sweeping how many chunks it retrieves. Going from 288 to 1,073 words of context — 3.7 times the context — multiplied the read by 3.6. Quadratic scaling would have predicted about fourteen. The lever is still sending less, but the reason is proportion rather than explosion.

One value of k cannot separate reading from writing

A single measurement of read time bundles two things: prefill, which grows with the context you sent, and decode, which grows with the answer the model writes. Neither is visible on its own. Project 2 separates them by sweeping how many chunks it retrieves — the answers stay roughly the same length while the context grows, so the slope of read time against context is prefill and the intercept is the decode floor.

On its corpus that floor is around three and a half seconds before a single retrieved word is read. If you are optimising context size against a read time dominated by an unchanging decode cost, you are tuning the smaller half and the numbers will barely move.

Running the evaluation

  • Fix the set and leave it alone. Its value comes from not moving. Edit it between runs and it measures nothing.
  • Score retrieval automatically. Recall@k and MRR need no judge — you have labels.
  • Score groundedness with a judge, carefully. This is the case where a judge is genuinely the last resort, so use a different model family, compare pairwise where possible, and calibrate against thirty hand-labelled examples once.
  • Re-run on every change. Chunk size, embedding model, index parameters, prompt, k — all of them are treatments.
  • Record the manifest. Embedding model and version, index parameters, chunk strategy, k, prompt version. A score six weeks old is uninterpretable without it.
