Building a Retrieval System That Works

Five stages, and the two that decide whether the other three matter.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a short pipeline with a long tail of ways to get it wrong. The stages are simple; the judgement is in the two that people treat as configuration.

Chunking is a modelling decision, not a parameter

The default advice — 512 tokens with 50 overlap — is a starting point that nobody should ship without looking at the output. The right question is not what size but what is a self-contained thought in this corpus?

Split on structure first
Headings, sections, functions
A chunk that ends mid-sentence embeds badly and reads worse. Character counts are a fallback, not a strategy.
Too small
Loses its referent
A paragraph saying "this threshold must not be exceeded" is useless if the threshold was defined two chunks earlier.
Too large
Averages several topics
The embedding represents none of them well, and retrieving it burns context on material the question did not ask about.

10-15%
So a passage spanning a boundary is not lost by both neighbours.

Small to match, larger to read
Retrieve the precise chunk, then optionally expand to its section for the model to read.
Worth knowing

Print twenty random chunks and read them before tuning anything downstream.

If you cannot tell what a chunk is about without its neighbours, neither can an embedding model. Every hour spent on embedding models or rerankers before doing this is spent on the wrong layer.

For exampleA team spent two weeks comparing embedding models on a technical corpus with flat retrieval quality throughout. Their PDF parser was splitting on page boundaries, so roughly a third of chunks began mid-sentence and ended mid-table. No embedding model can rescue that, and ten minutes of reading would have found it.

What actually makes retrieval fail

In rough order of how often it is the real cause:

  • The chunk containing the answer was never retrieved. Nothing downstream can fix this, and it is by far the commonest failure.
  • It was retrieved but ranked ninth. Attention is weakest mid-context, so a correct chunk buried in position nine is close to not retrieving it.
  • The question needs two chunks that live far apart. Single-shot retrieval cannot combine them — this is the case the agentic lesson addresses.
  • The corpus does not contain the answer. Worth establishing early, because everything else is wasted effort if so.
  • The model ignored the context. Real, and much rarer than people assume. Check the first four before concluding this.
Diagnose in that order, and cheaplyFor a failing question, look at what was retrieved before looking at the answer. If the right chunk is absent, it is a retrieval problem. If it is present but ranked low, it is a ranking problem. If it is present and first and the answer is still wrong, only then is it a generation problem. Three different fixes, and people reach for the third by default.

Retrieval: cheap, wide, then precise

The architecture that works, and the reasoning behind each step:

Retrieve wide
50 by keyword, 50 by vector
Independent failure modes, so coverage is better than either alone. Cheap at this stage.
Fuse by rank
Reciprocal rank fusion
Two lines of code, no score normalisation, no tuning. Reliably beats either input list.
Rerank the shortlist
Cross-encoder over ~100
Scores query and document together rather than separately. Far more accurate, and only affordable because the set is small.
Send few
Top 3 to 5
Precision beats volume. Distractors actively degrade the answer rather than being politely ignored.

Generation, and making the answer checkable

  • Require citations to the retrieved chunks. Not a bibliography — a mapping from claim to source span, so a human can verify one sentence without re-reading the corpus.
  • Licence refusal explicitly. "If the context does not contain the answer, say so" costs one line and is the difference between a wrong answer and no answer.
  • Define the empty-retrieval path. When nothing relevant came back, the model must not fall through to its parameters. This is where grounded systems quietly stop being grounded.
  • Put the retrieved material at the start or the end. Never buried in the middle of a long prompt.
Worth knowing

A grounded system stops being grounded the moment retrieval returns nothing and the model answers anyway.

The grounding lives in the pipeline, not in the model. If your prompt does not explicitly handle the empty case, the model falls back to its parameters and produces a fluent, uncited, unverifiable answer that looks exactly like the grounded ones.

For exampleA support assistant answers a question about a product discontinued before its training cutoff. Retrieval returned nothing, because the product is not in the knowledge base. The answer is confident, plausible, and drawn entirely from training data — indistinguishable in tone from the answers that were genuinely retrieved.
