Lesson 5 of 5

Recommendation Systems, and the Loop They Sit In

The one system in this course whose training data is its own past output.

Recommenders are the most widely deployed machine learning systems in existence and the ones whose published accuracy numbers correspond least well to whether they work. Both facts have the same cause, and it is not the algorithm. A recommender is trained on data that a recommender produced, evaluated against behaviour it shaped, and judged on a metric that stops meaning what it meant the moment anyone optimises it.

The two families, and why every real system is both

Collaborative filtering
People like you liked this
Uses only the interaction matrix — who engaged with what. It knows nothing about the items themselves, which is its strength: it finds affinities nobody could have described in advance.
Content-based recommendation
This is like what you liked
Uses features of the items — text, tags, an embedding. It can recommend something nobody has touched yet, which is precisely where collaborative filtering has nothing to say.
The hybrid
Content for the cold, collaborative for the warm
Almost every production system. Content-based handles new items and new users; collaborative takes over once there is enough interaction to be worth using.
The matrix
Overwhelmingly empty
A million users and a hundred thousand items is 100 billion cells, of which perhaps 0.01% are filled. Sparsity is not an inconvenience here, it is the defining property of the problem.

What the data is actually telling you

Explicit feedback — a rating, a star, a thumbs down — is unambiguous and almost absent, because most people never rate anything. Implicit feedback — a click, a watch, a purchase — is abundant and means considerably less than it appears to.

Worth knowing

A click is not a like, and a non-click is not a dislike. Most of the empty cells mean “never shown”.

Treating unobserved interactions as negative examples is the standard shortcut and it is wrong in a specific direction: the items a user did not interact with are overwhelmingly items they were never offered, and what they were offered was decided by the previous version of the recommender. The zeros in your matrix are a record of a past policy's choices, not of user preference — which makes this the Block 1 missing-data problem in its most consequential form. The data is missing not at random, and the mechanism generating the missingness is your own system.

For exampleA user has never watched a documentary. Two explanations: they dislike documentaries, or the homepage has never shown them one because an early model decided they were a comedy viewer and every subsequent model trained on the resulting history. The matrix looks identical in both cases and the correct action is opposite.

Cold start, in three flavours

  • New user. No history, so collaborative filtering has nothing. Ask a few questions, use whatever context exists, or serve popular items and accept that the first session is weak.
  • New item. Nobody has touched it, so it cannot be recommended, so nobody touches it. Content features are the only way out, and without them a catalogue addition is invisible indefinitely.
  • New system. The one people forget. With no interaction history at all, the collaborative half cannot start, and the choices you make to bootstrap it become the data everything afterwards learns from.

Popularity bias, and the loop that produces it

Popular items are recommended more, which gets them engaged with more, which makes them more popular. Nothing in the objective asks for this — it falls out of training on data the system generated. Left alone it narrows the catalogue in front of every user and concentrates engagement on a shrinking set of items, and the metrics improve throughout, because the metric is engagement and engagement is what is being concentrated.

Why the offline number does not predict the online one

This is the part that surprises people arriving from ordinary supervised learning. You hold out interactions, train, and measure error against them — and the resulting figure is a famously poor predictor of what happens when the system is deployed. Three reasons, each sufficient on its own.

You are ranking, not predicting
The metric is the wrong shape
Nobody sees a predicted rating. They see the top ten. Error averaged over all held-out interactions is dominated by items that will never be shown, so it can improve while the top of the list gets worse. Ranking metrics — precision at k, normalised discounted cumulative gain — at least measure the part anyone experiences.
The held-out data came from the old policy
You cannot observe the counterfactual
Your test set records what users did when shown the previous model's choices. A new model that would have recommended something better has no evidence for it in that data, because nobody was ever shown it. Offline evaluation systematically favours models that agree with the one already running.
Position and presentation dominate
The slot is doing the work
The top slot gets clicked far more than the fifth regardless of what is in it. Without correcting for it you measure position, and conclude that the model improved when the layout changed.
The clearest Goodhart case in the whole course

Engagement is chosen as the objective because it correlates with value: people spend time on things they like. Optimise it hard and the correlation is what breaks first. What reliably increases engagement is not what people value most, it is what is hardest to stop doing — and a system with enough capacity finds that distinction long before anyone reviewing a dashboard does.

The measurement lesson's remedy applies directly and is unglamorous: pair the target with guardrails chosen in advance and permitted to veto. Catalogue coverage, repeat-visit rate at a week, explicit negative feedback, the share of engagement concentrated in the top one per cent of items. A recommender improving on engagement while every one of those degrades is not improving, and only the guardrails can say so.

What to do about the loop

  • Log what was shown, not only what was clicked. Without the slate, you cannot tell an item that was rejected from one that was never offered, and every analysis afterwards is guesswork.
  • Reserve a fraction of traffic for exploration. A small share of randomised or deliberately diverse recommendations is the only source of evidence about items the current policy would never show. It costs measurable engagement and it is what keeps the training data from being purely self-generated.
  • Measure coverage as a first-class metric. What share of the catalogue was recommended to anyone this week? A number that falls steadily is the loop closing, visible before the engagement number turns.
  • Test online. The offline number is for filtering obviously bad candidates. The decision belongs to an experiment with real traffic, and the deployment lesson's interleaved comparison is unusually well suited here, since each user acts as their own control.
