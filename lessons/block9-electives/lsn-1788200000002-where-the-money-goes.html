Lesson 3 of 6

Where the Money Goes

Block 0 taught routing, which is architecture. This is the measurement — attributing spend, and the four places it hides.

The vocabulary block taught cheap-first architecture: route simple work to a small model and escalate when it fails. That is the design decision. This lesson is the part that comes after — knowing what you actually spent and on what, which is a measurement problem and is almost never solved by looking at the invoice.

An invoice is not an attribution

A monthly total tells you the size of the problem and nothing about its shape. The question worth answering is which requests, which features and which users produced it, and that requires recording cost per request at the point the request happens, because it cannot be reconstructed afterwards.

Cached against uncached
The number that says whether caching works
Cache reads cost roughly a tenth of normal input. If that ratio is not visible per request, a cache that silently stopped working looks exactly like one that never existed, except on the total.
Cost per successful outcome
Not cost per call
A cheap model that needs three attempts and an escalation is not cheap. Attribute the whole chain to the outcome, or a routing strategy that is losing money will look like one that is saving it.
The long tail
Where it usually is
Spend is rarely uniform. A small number of users, features or question types typically dominate, and a median tells you nothing about them. Look at the distribution and specifically at the top percentile.
Worth knowing

The expensive request is usually the one that failed and was retried.

Cost dashboards are built around successful calls because those are the ones that produce something to attribute. The retries, the truncated generations, the escalations and the runs that hit a turn cap are all paid for in full and produce nothing, and they are frequently a large fraction of spend. Any measurement that counts only what worked will understate the bill and — worse — will hide the fact that the overspend is a reliability problem rather than a pricing one.

The four places it hides

  • Retries and timeouts — paid in full, produce nothing, and Block 8 showed a timed-out call may have been billed twice.
  • Context growth — history re-sent every turn means cost grows with the square of a conversation's length. The single biggest driver of surprise bills.
  • Broken caching — fails open and silently. A timestamp near the front of a prompt takes the hit rate to zero and nothing errors.
  • Reasoning tokens — billed as output and invisible in the answer. A model that thinks for eight hundred tokens before writing forty has charged for eight hundred and forty.
Set a ceiling that stops something, not one that emails you

A budget alert is a notification that money has already been spent. The control that works is a hard cap in the path — a per-key spend limit, a token budget per request, a turn cap on a loop — because the failure mode being guarded against is a runaway loop at three in the morning, and that is precisely when nobody is reading alerts.

1. Why is cost per call a misleading metric?

It ignores infrastructure costs beyond the model
A cheap model needing three attempts and an escalation is not cheap — the whole chain must be attributed to the outcome, or a losing strategy looks like a winning one
Providers price calls differently by time of day

2. Why does a broken prompt cache go unnoticed?

Cache metrics are usually sampled rather than complete
It fails open — the requests still succeed and the answers are still correct, so the only symptom is the bill
Providers do not expose cache hit rates

3. What kind of spending control actually prevents a runaway loop?

A budget alert configured on the provider dashboard
A hard cap in the request path — a per-key limit or a token budget — because an alert is a notification that the money is already gone
