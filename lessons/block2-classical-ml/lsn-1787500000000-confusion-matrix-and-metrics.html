Lesson 1 of 4

The Confusion Matrix and What Comes Out of It

Four numbers, and every classification metric you will ever meet is built from them.

Almost every argument about model quality is really an argument about which of four numbers matters most. Get the four straight and the metrics stop being a vocabulary test.

The four cells

The metrics, and the question each answers

Accuracy
All correct, over everything
Meaningless when one class dominates. Always compare it against the majority-class baseline before saying it aloud.
Precision
Of what I flagged, how much was real
Reads the predicted-positive column. Low precision means wasted effort chasing false alarms.
Recall (sensitivity)
Of what was real, how much did I catch
Reads the actual-positive row. Low recall means missed cases.
F1
The harmonic mean of the two
One number when you must have one. It hides the trade-off rather than resolving it, so report the pair alongside.
Specificity
Of the genuinely negative, how many did I leave alone
The mirror of recall. Matters when false alarms are the expensive error.
Precision and recall are not properties of the modelThey are properties of the model at a chosen threshold. The same model can be tuned to catch 93% of failures with 770 false alarms, or 44% with 77. Quoting a precision figure without the threshold it was measured at is quoting half a result.
Worth knowing

Precision reads a column of the matrix; recall reads a row.

Precision asks what fraction of your positive predictions were correct — it divides by everything you flagged. Recall asks what fraction of the real positives you found — it divides by everything that was actually positive. Different denominators, which is why they trade against each other.

For exampleA turbine model flags 392 machines, of which 124 genuinely fail. Precision is 124/392 = 32% — most call-outs are wasted. There were 187 real failures, so recall is 124/187 = 66% — a third were missed. Same model, same run, two very different complaints from two different departments.

ROC and the area under it

A classifier outputs probabilities, so it is really a family of classifiers — one for every threshold. The ROC curve — receiver operating characteristic, a name inherited from radar operators in the 1940s — plots how the model behaves across all of them.

The vertical axis is the true positive rate, which is recall. The horizontal axis is the false positive rate, the fraction of genuine negatives wrongly flagged. Sweeping the threshold from strict to permissive traces a curve from the bottom-left corner to the top-right.

AUC — area under the curve — collapses that whole curve into one number between 0 and 1. It has an interpretation worth remembering: it is the probability that the model scores a randomly chosen positive case higher than a randomly chosen negative one. An AUC of 0.91 means it gets that ordering right 91 times in 100.

Why AUC is useful and why it is not enoughIt is threshold-free, so it measures ranking ability rather than any particular operating point — which makes it good for comparing models before you have chosen a threshold. That same property is its weakness: it says nothing about whether the threshold you will actually deploy is any good, and it is optimistic on heavily imbalanced data because the false positive rate has an enormous denominator.
Worth knowing

AUC is the probability that the model ranks a random positive above a random negative.

It measures ordering, not decisions. That makes it the right tool for comparing two models before a threshold exists, and the wrong tool for judging whether a deployed system is behaving acceptably.

For exampleTwo fraud models both score 0.87 AUC. One is usable and one is not — at the threshold that catches 80% of fraud, the first raises 200 false alarms a day and the second raises 4,000. The AUC could not have told you that, because it averaged over thresholds you would never choose.

When to use the precision-recall curve instead

On heavily imbalanced data, prefer a precision-recall curve. The reason is the denominator: the false positive rate divides by the number of genuine negatives, and when negatives outnumber positives a hundred to one, even a large absolute number of false alarms produces a small false positive rate. The ROC curve looks reassuring while your team drowns in call-outs.

The precision-recall curve has no such comfort. Precision divides by what you flagged, so false alarms hurt it directly and visibly.

Regression metrics, briefly

MAE
Mean absolute error
Average distance from the truth, in the original units. Robust to outliers and easy to explain to anyone.
RMSE
Root mean squared error
Penalises large errors disproportionately. Use it when one big miss is worse than several small ones.
R-squared
Proportion of variance explained
Comparable across problems, and easy to over-read — a high value on a trivially predictable target means nothing.
MAPE
Mean absolute percentage error
Percentage terms, so it travels well between stakeholders. Breaks badly when true values approach zero.
Report the error in units someone can act on"RMSE 0.34" means nothing to an operations manager. "On average we are out by about four days, and occasionally by three weeks" is the same information in a form that supports a decision. Translate before presenting, every time.

1. Precision and recall differ in what, fundamentally?

Precision is for balanced data and recall for imbalanced
Their denominators — precision divides by what you flagged, recall by what was actually positive
Precision is computed on training data and recall on test data

2. What does an AUC of 0.91 actually mean?

The model is correct 91% of the time
It ranks a randomly chosen positive above a randomly chosen negative 91 times in 100
91% of positives are caught at the default threshold

3. Why prefer a precision-recall curve on heavily imbalanced data?

It is faster to compute
The ROC false positive rate divides by a huge number of negatives, so it looks reassuring while false alarms pile up
ROC curves cannot be computed when classes are imbalanced

4. Someone quotes a model's precision as 82%. What is missing?

The recall figure
The threshold it was measured at — precision is a property of the model at an operating point, not of the model
The size of the test set
