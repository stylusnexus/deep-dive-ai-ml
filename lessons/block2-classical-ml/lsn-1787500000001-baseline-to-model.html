From Baseline to Model

A ladder of increasing complexity, climbed only as far as the evidence justifies.

The temptation on a new problem is to reach for the strongest thing you know. The disciplined approach is a ladder, and it is faster in practice because most rungs take minutes and each one tells you something the next cannot.

The ladder

0 · Majority class
Seconds
Predict the most common answer for everything. This is the number every later result is judged against.
1 · One rule
Minutes
Threshold the single most predictive feature. Surprisingly often within a point or two of everything above it.
2 · Logistic regression
Minutes
Interpretable coefficients, fast, deployable anywhere. The real baseline for tabular problems.
3 · Random forest
Minutes
Strong, needs almost no tuning, resistant to overfitting. Usually your first genuinely competitive score.
4 · Gradient boosting
Hours, with care
Higher ceiling, requires early stopping and a learning rate you chose deliberately.
5 · Neural network
Days
For tabular data this is rarely the answer, and the next lesson explains why.
Worth knowing

Climb the ladder until the gain stops justifying the cost, then stop.

Each rung costs more to build, tune, explain and operate than the one below it. A two-point gain that turns an interpretable model into an opaque one is frequently a bad trade, and it is a trade nobody makes explicitly if the ladder is skipped.

For exampleOn the turbine data, logistic regression reaches 71% recall cross-validated. A tuned boosted model reaches 74%. Three points, in exchange for a model you cannot explain to a maintenance manager, that needs a serving runtime, and that will need retuning when the data drifts. Sometimes worth it. Not automatically.

Comparing honestly

Most model comparisons are quietly unfair, in ways that all favour the newer model. Four rules make them fair:

  • Same split, same folds, same seed. A different random split can move a score by more than a real modelling improvement.
  • Same preprocessing pipeline. If one model gets scaled and imputed features and another gets raw ones, you are comparing pipelines, not models.
  • Same metric, chosen before you looked. Picking the metric that flatters your preferred model afterwards is the most common form of self-deception in this work.
  • Report the spread, not just the mean. A model at 74% ± 4 and one at 71% ± 3 are not clearly different, and presenting them as though they were is a claim the data does not support.
Worth knowing

Two models whose confidence intervals overlap are not distinguishable, however different their means look.

Cross-validation folds vary. A three-point difference with four points of spread on each side is consistent with the models being equally good, and reporting it as an improvement is a claim the evidence does not support.

For exampleA team ships a boosted model over logistic regression on a 2.8-point gain, then spends a quarter maintaining a serving stack for it. Re-run with different fold seeds, the gap is 0.4 points. The original difference was fold assignment, not modelling.

What to do when nothing beats the baseline

This happens, and it is information rather than failure. Work through it in order:

  • Is the signal there at all? Plot the strongest feature against the target. If the classes overlap almost entirely, no algorithm will separate them.
  • Is the target well defined? "Failure within 90 days" may be recorded inconsistently, which puts a ceiling on any model.
  • Is there a feature you have not built? Ratios, differences and time-since events frequently carry signal that raw columns do not — hours per year of age beat both hours and age on the turbine data.
  • Is the problem framed correctly? Predicting which week a failure falls in may be answerable when predicting whether it happens is not.
  • Is it genuinely unpredictable? A defensible finding, and much cheaper to deliver in week two than in month six.
A negative result delivered early is a real deliverable"We cannot predict this from the data we have, and here is what we would need" is a better outcome than six months of model-tuning that ends in the same place. The intake sheet asks for a kill condition precisely so this conversation is easy to have.

Feature engineering, which beats model selection

On tabular problems, a good feature usually moves the number more than a better algorithm does. The productive kinds:

Ratios and rates
Intensity, not totals
Hours per year of age separates a hard-worked young turbine from a coasting old one. Neither raw column carries that.
Differences from a norm
Deviation, not level
Temperature relative to the site median matters more than temperature.
Time since an event
Recency
Days since last inspection, days since last fault.
Aggregates over a group
Context
This turbine's vibration against its site's average.
Missingness indicators
Absence as signal
Already covered in Block 1, and worth repeating because it is so often left out.
