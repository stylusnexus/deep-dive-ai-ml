Time Series, and Why Random Splits Lie

Routinely skipped, in courses whose own case studies are time-series problems.

Predictive maintenance, demand forecasting, ride-share volume — courses use all three as examples and rarely teach the thing they have in common. Every one is ordered in time, and ordered data breaks the standard workflow in a way that produces excellent scores and worthless models.

Why the random split fails

A random split scatters rows from every period into both halves. The model trains on Tuesday and Thursday and is tested on Wednesday — surrounded, on both sides, by data it has already seen.

Worth knowing

A random split on time-ordered data trains on the future and tests on the past.

Real deployment only ever asks about the future. A model validated by interpolating between known points has been asked an easier question than the one it will face, and the gap between the two shows up as a launch-day collapse.

For exampleA demand model scores 0.91 R-squared on a random split and 0.62 when re-validated on a time-ordered one. Nothing changed but the split. The first number was measuring how well it could fill gaps in a period it had already learned.

Walk-forward validation

The time-series equivalent of cross-validation. Train on everything up to a point, test on what comes next, then move the boundary forward and repeat. You get several estimates, each of which is honest about direction.

Expanding window
Train set grows each fold
Uses all history. The default when older data stays relevant.
Sliding window
Fixed-length train set that moves
Discards old data deliberately. Right when the process changes and history becomes misleading.
Gap between train and test
Often necessary
If your prediction is used a week ahead, leave a week's gap — otherwise you are testing with information the deployed model will not have.
The gap is the part people leave outYou predict failures 90 days ahead. If your test period starts the day after your training period ends, the model is being scored on a task it will never be given — it had data up to yesterday and is predicting tomorrow, when in production it will have data from 90 days ago. Insert the gap and the honest score is usually noticeably lower.

Features that only exist in time series

Lags
The value n periods ago
Yesterday's reading, last week's total. Frequently the single strongest predictor, and the one to build first.
Rolling statistics
Mean, max, standard deviation over a window
A seven-day rolling mean smooths noise; a rolling standard deviation captures instability, which is often the real signal.
Differences
Change since last period
Converts a level into a movement, which is usually what matters.
Time since an event
Days since last fault or service
Encodes recency directly rather than hoping the model infers it.
Calendar features
Day of week, month, holiday
Cheap, and they capture seasonality no lag will.
Every rolling feature is a leakage opportunityA rolling mean computed over the whole series before splitting includes future values in every row. The window must only ever look backwards, and the calculation must happen inside the fold. This is the single commonest bug in time-series pipelines, and it produces a beautiful score.
Worth knowing

Any feature computed across the whole series before splitting has put the future into your training rows.

Rolling means, normalisations, target encodings and imputations all aggregate over rows. Compute them before the split and each training row carries information from dates after it — invisible in the metrics and fatal in production.

For exampleA team normalises a sensor column by its overall mean and standard deviation, then splits by time. Every training row is now scaled using statistics that include the test period. The model looks excellent, ships, and degrades immediately — the scaler it needs at inference cannot see the future it was calibrated against.

What to check before modelling

  • Plot it. Trend, seasonality, level shifts and obvious breaks are visible in one chart and invisible in summary statistics.
  • Check the sampling. Irregular intervals, missing periods and duplicated timestamps all break lag features silently.
  • Look for regime changes. A sensor recalibration or a process change means older data may be actively misleading, which is the argument for a sliding window.
  • Establish the naive baseline. "Tomorrow equals today" is remarkably hard to beat on many series, and it is the number your model must clear.
The naive forecast is the majority-class baseline of time seriesPredicting that the next value equals the last one — or the value from the same day last week, for seasonal data — costs nothing and beats a surprising number of models. Compute it first, and if your model cannot clear it, you have learned that early and cheaply.

✅ Check Yourself

1. Why does a random split inflate scores on time-ordered data?

Random splits always produce optimistic estimates
Test points sit between training points, so the model interpolates rather than forecasting — an easier task than production asks
Time series have more autocorrelation than tabular data

2. Your model will be used to predict 90 days ahead. What must your validation include?

A larger test set to cover the period
A 90-day gap between the end of training and the start of testing, matching the information the deployed model will have
Only the most recent 90 days as training data

3. You compute a rolling mean over the full series, then split by time. What went wrong?

Rolling means should be computed after modelling
Every training row now includes information from dates after it — the future is in your training data
The window length was probably too long

4. What is the naive baseline for a time series?

The mean of the training period
The next value equals the last one, or the same period last season
A linear trend fitted to the whole series
