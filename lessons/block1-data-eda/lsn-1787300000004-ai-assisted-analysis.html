Lesson 5 of 6

AI-Assisted Analysis, and Where It Lies to You

The original syllabus promises 'Python with generative AI'. Here's the honest version.

Using a model to help with data work is genuinely productive, and it fails in specific, predictable ways. Knowing which is which is the difference between a real speed-up and confidently wrong analysis.

Where it reliably helps

  • Syntax you'd otherwise look up. Reshaping, multi-level groupbys, the exact seaborn incantation. High value, low risk — the code either runs and does the visible thing or it doesn't.
  • Boilerplate. Plot scaffolding, pipeline structure, docstrings.
  • Explaining an error. Pasting a traceback is often faster than searching.
  • Reviewing your approach. "Here's my cleaning sequence — what have I not considered?" It's good at generating candidate concerns for you to check.
  • Naming and structure. Turning a working mess into something readable.

Where it lies

Invented methods
Plausible, non-existent APIs
A pandas method that sounds exactly right and doesn't exist. Easy to catch — it errors.
Silently wrong logic
Runs, wrong answer
The dangerous one. A groupby on the wrong key, a merge that drops rows, an off-by-one on a date window. No error, plausible output.
Confident domain claims
Statistics stated as fact
"A variance inflation factor (VIF) above 5 indicates multicollinearity" — the sort of threshold that's conventional rather than true, delivered with certainty.
Context it cannot have
About your specific data
It hasn't seen your rows. Any claim about what your data contains is inference from the column names.
The failure that actually costs youNot the hallucinated function — that errors immediately. It's the merge that silently drops 400 rows because the join key had different whitespace on each side. The code runs, the output looks fine, and your analysis is wrong. **After every AI-suggested transform, check the row count and the null counts.** Two lines, and it catches most of this.

A workflow that holds up

1. You decide
The question and the approach
The model is bad at knowing what matters in your business. That judgement is the part you're actually paid for.
2. It drafts
The code
Where it's genuinely fast.
3. You verify
Shape, nulls, a spot check
Before and after. Non-negotiable.
4. You interpret
What it means
The model will happily explain a result it cannot see the context for.

The trap specific to data work

Ask a model to "clean this dataset" and it will produce something reasonable-looking: fill the nulls, drop the duplicates, encode the categoricals. Every one of those is a decision with consequences, and it made all of them for you, invisibly.

In the Block 1 data, the reasonable-looking action — filling vibration with the median — is precisely wrong, because the values are missing not at random. No model can know that from the column names. It requires knowing that vibration sensors fail on shaking turbines, which is domain knowledge you had to go and get.

The line worth holdingLet it write the code. Don't let it make the decisions. Every entry on your cleaning-decisions list should be one you can defend, and 'the model suggested it' is not a defence you'd want to give to an operations manager whose maintenance schedule you got wrong.

The connection to everything else

This is the same principle as the rest of the program arriving early: verify rather than trust, hold something back that the optimiser can't see, and don't let the thing producing the work also be the thing judging it. Blocks 5 and 6 formalise it. Here it's just good practice.

Worth knowing

Let it write the code. Do not let it make the decisions.

It is genuinely fast at syntax and boilerplate. It has not seen your data, does not know how it was collected, and cannot know which of several defensible cleaning choices is right for your problem.
