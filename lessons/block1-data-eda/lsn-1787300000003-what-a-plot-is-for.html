What a Plot Is Actually For

Exploration with intent — five questions worth asking, and the charts that answer them.

Exploratory analysis becomes a ritual very easily: produce a grid of histograms, note that things look roughly as expected, move on. A plot earns its place only when you would do something different depending on what it shows.

Five questions with decisions attached

What shape is it?
Histogram
Skewed, bimodal, bounded? Decides whether the mean is a meaningful summary and whether a transform would help.
Does it relate to the target?
Box plot by class
Decides which features are worth keeping before you fit anything.
Are features redundant?
Correlation heatmap
Two near-identical columns destabilise linear models. Decides what to combine or drop.
Do the groups really differ?
Bar chart with error bars
Decides whether an apparent group effect is real or sample noise.
Is the missingness informative?
Rate by missing flag
Decides whether the flag becomes a feature.

Read the gap between mean and median

Draw both on a histogram. Where the mean sits noticeably to the right of the median, the variable is right-skewed — a long tail pulling the average up. It follows that "the average turbine" is a misleading summary, and that a linear model may do better on the log of the variable than on the variable.

The two mistakes that recur

Correlation only sees straight linesPearson correlation measures linear association. A strong curved relationship — performance rising then falling with temperature, say — can show a correlation near zero. Never conclude 'no relationship' from a low correlation without looking at the scatter plot.
A bar chart without error bars is an assertionSite A fails at 17% and site B at 15%. Real difference, or forty turbines' worth of noise? Without confidence intervals the chart cannot tell you, and it will be read as though it had. This is your statistics course's sample-size point, applied to the most common chart in business analysis.

Confounding, in one example

In the Block 1 data one site has both a higher failure rate and older turbines. Is the site risky, or is it just old? The chart cannot separate those, and reporting "site X is our problem site" on that basis would send maintenance budget to the wrong place.

The honest answer is to compare like with like — failure rate by site within age bands — or to let a model with both features estimate their separate contributions. What you must not do is read a raw group difference as a causal claim about the group.

What the exploration pass should produce

Not a folder of charts. A short list of findings that change what you do next: which features carry signal, which are redundant, which groups differ, and where you're uncertain. In Block 1 that list is five lines long and every line shapes the modelling that follows.
