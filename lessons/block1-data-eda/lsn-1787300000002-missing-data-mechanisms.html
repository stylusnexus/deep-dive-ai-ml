Missing Data: The Mechanism Matters More Than the Amount

Why 'fill it with the median' is a decision about the world, not a default.

The standard reflex on encountering gaps is to fill them with the column's mean or median and move on. Whether that's reasonable depends entirely on why the values are absent — and that question has three standard answers with very different consequences.

The three mechanisms

MCAR
Missing completely at random
The gap is unrelated to anything — a dropped packet, a corrupted row. Filling is relatively safe and you mainly lose statistical power.
MAR
Missing at random, given other columns
Missingness is explained by data you *do* have. One site's sensors fail more often. Fill using those other columns and you can do well.
MNAR
Missing not at random
Missingness depends on the missing value itself. High-vibration sensors drop out *because* of the vibration. Filling actively introduces bias.
You cannot test for MNAR directlyBy definition you don't have the values that would prove it. What you can do is compare rows where a value is missing against rows where it's present, on every *other* column. Be careful reading the result: if those groups differ systematically you have ruled out MCAR, and nothing more. A difference explained by columns you hold is MAR; MNAR is when the missingness depends on the absent value itself, and no observed column can show you that. Which of the two it is comes from knowing how the data was collected, not from the table — so this test tells you when to go and ask, rather than what the answer is.

The Block 1 case

Vibration readings are missing on about 7% of turbines. Comparing those rows against the rest: the missing group has a failure rate of 24% against 14.9% for the rest. Missingness is strongly associated with the outcome.

The mechanism is physical — the sensors fail most on the turbines shaking hardest. So the missing values are disproportionately the high ones. Fill them with the median and you replace the most alarming readings in the dataset with typical ones, systematically hiding the risk you were hired to detect.

Worth knowing

Why a value is missing matters more than how many are missing.

Under MNAR — missing not at random — the absent values are systematically different from the present ones. Filling them with a typical value replaces the unusual cases with ordinary ones, which is precisely backwards.

For exampleVibration readings are missing on 7% of turbines. Those turbines fail at 24% against 14.9% for the rest, because the sensors drop out on the machines shaking hardest. Fill with the median and you have replaced your most alarming readings with unremarkable ones, hiding the risk you were hired to find.

The move that turns a problem into a feature

Add a binary column recording that the value was missing, before filling anything. It costs one column and preserves the signal that the imputation is about to destroy. In the Block 1 pipeline, that flag ends up among the more predictive features — the absence of data is data.

The order that mattersRecord the flag, then impute — and impute inside a pipeline, after the split. Fitting an imputer on the full dataset means the training data has seen the test set's statistics. It's one of the most common leaks in practice precisely because it doesn't feel like model fitting.

Choosing a fill

  • Median — robust to skew and outliers. The sensible default for numeric columns.
  • Mean — only for roughly symmetric distributions. Skew drags it.
  • Most frequent — for categoricals, though a literal "missing" category is often more honest.
  • Model-based (KNN, iterative) — predicts the gap from other columns. Genuinely better under MAR, and slower and easier to leak with.
  • Leave it — gradient boosting implementations handle missing values natively and often do better than any fill you'd choose.
