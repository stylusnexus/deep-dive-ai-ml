Not usually taught as its own module. It should be.

Most of this course's evaluation material was already written, scattered by design: the vocabulary in Block 0, the procedure in engineering practice, the checklist in artifacts/sop/measurement.md. Two of the four topics here - why public benchmarks mislead, and building a private eval set - are taught in core-vocabulary/eval-battery, and LLM-as-judge is taught in engineering-practice/models-judging-models. Restating them here would have been padding, so this block is a door plus the two genuine gaps.

  • Where evaluation lives - the map, and the order that saves a week ✅
  • Eval data you can trust: labels that follow from the rules, not from your opinion ✅
  • One set, many models: the comparison, and the output budget that silently breaks it ✅
  • Rubrics and blind reads - the two mechanics that make a judge usable ✅
  • Is the difference real? - pairing, per-arm distribution, Bessel's correction, A/B discipline and the peeking problem, with the numbers simulated ✅
  • Judging a conversation - scoring the trajectory, since the failure is rarely in the turn you graded ✅

6 lessons in lessons/block5-evaluation/ and 2 notebooks in notebooks/05-evaluation/. The statistics notebook needs no model, network or API key - every figure the lesson quotes is produced by running it. The second tests an essay's claim that annotating a graph's triples makes a model answer better: one synthetic graph, three formats, gold answers computed from the graph, run against whatever local models are reachable. On the one model available here the annotated format did not help. A battery across several providers still needs keys this repository does not assume.

Exit: Your own eval set, run across ≥6 models, with a defensible ranking - and a non-completion column, without which the ranking is not defensible.

Block 7 · Training & Fine-Tuning - 1.5 weeks

Rarely taught at all.

  • When fine-tuning is the wrong answer (usually - prompt, then RAG, then this) ✅
  • Dataset construction, and how little data you actually need ✅
  • LoRA / QLoRA vs full fine-tune, locally on the M4 Max ✅
  • Supervised versus reinforcement fine-tuning ✅ - the objective decides what you get. Supervised tuning imitates answers; reinforcement tuning (GRPO and relatives) optimises against a reward or a verifier, which is how reasoning behaviour is trained rather than copied. Block 0 teaches what a reasoning model is and this is where it gets made.
  • The four ways to shrink a model ✅ - quantization, pruning, distillation and low-rank factorisation as one family; which compose (pruning then distillation as repair) and which are alternatives; and that compression does not remove capability evenly, so it needs slice evaluation rather than an aggregate.

6 lessons in lessons/block7-training/ and 2 notebooks in notebooks/07-finetuning/: the checks that precede a run, and the analysis of one that was actually performed.

The run is done. A LoRA adapter was trained on Apple Silicon against Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct-4bit - 965 examples, 600 iterations, 4.16 GB peak, minutes on one laptop - and evaluated against the base model with and without policies, and against the lookup table. It lost to the dictionary (0.393 against 0.458) and scored zero on the judgement cases the base model got a quarter of, because it learned to reproduce the rule including the rule's blind spot. The dataset check in notebook 01 predicted this before any compute was spent.

Still open: quantization for serving, and publishing the adapter to the stylusnexus HF org, which is an outward-facing action and deliberately not automated.

Closing · The Future of Work

Different in kind from everything else here.

The only chapter that argues rather than instructs, and it says so in its first paragraph. One lesson separates what the 2026 labour evidence actually measures - no aggregate employment effect, a 9-16% relative decline in junior hiring with senior roles unaffected, productivity gains largest for the least experienced and negative past the model's frontier. The second makes an argument from it: that junior work was simultaneously output and apprenticeship, and automating the first removes the second by accident.

The counter-argument is in the lesson rather than omitted from it. Every previous automation of professional work produced this same prediction and each time the apprenticeship reformed around the tool. The historical prior is against the thesis and the chapter says so.

  • What the evidence actually shows ✅
  • The apprenticeship problem ✅

Exit: none. It is an essay, and the reader is meant to disagree with it.
